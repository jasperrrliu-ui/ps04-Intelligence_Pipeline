# What Should a Beginner-Facing AI Stage App Promise?

## Opening

**Narration:** Imagine describing a scene in a few sentences and seeing a stage concept appear. That could be a useful way to explore design. But professional stage work also depends on the story, the venue, the performers, and collaboration. So what should an AI stage-design app actually promise? [03-c1][03-c2]

**Visual suggestion:** Show a short text prompt beside a simple stage sketch, with a label: “Starting point for exploration.”

## Make the hidden questions visible

**Narration:** Lighting designers use light to guide attention, shape atmosphere, and define a story space. Interviews with Walter Trarbach and Bruno Poet describe how lighting and sound contribute to a live audience’s experience. Poet also emphasizes the choices around darkness and where the audience should look. These are professional perspectives, not app evaluations. [03-c1]

A beginner-facing app could ask: Where does the scene happen? Which character or object should draw attention? What changes during the scene? What should remain unseen? These prompts are a product idea based on the collected sources; their value for learning still needs to be tested. [03-c4]

**Visual suggestion:** Animate four questions appearing next to a simplified stage plan. Do not show the software making an unchangeable final decision.

## Treat the venue as part of the prompt

**Narration:** Scenic designer Andrew Cohen describes designing for both the play and the theatre. Layout and rigging affect what can be placed overhead, how scenery moves, and how actors remain visible. A text description alone cannot capture every physical condition. [03-c2]

An early app might let users describe a simplified space, place actors and objects, and compare editable options. It could explain how a suggestion relates to the story. That is a hypothesis for a learning scaffold—not a tested workflow, engineering plan, or replacement for production collaboration.

**Visual suggestion:** Toggle between two simple stage layouts and show how an entrance or scenic object changes the available choices.

## Use research carefully

**Narration:** The AuraLuxMuse paper describes a prototype that attempts to generate lighting cues related to music and design preferences. Its authors report experiments and discuss limits in representing lighting parameters, aligning audio and cues, incorporating preferences, and evaluating results. This research does not show that one model can turn any script into a complete design for a real theatre. [03-c3]

An app could offer several visual directions, explain which user choices shaped them, and invite revision. It should call those directions suggestions, not expert-approved designs. Whether they help people learn or only produce attractive images is an open evaluation question. [03-c4]

**Visual suggestion:** Label an output “Option A” and show controls for changing focus, color, and actor position. Add a note that technical suitability needs professional review.

## Keep uncertainty and people in the process

**Narration:** Designers work with performers, directors, technicians, and the conditions of a venue. Interviews show examples of adaptation and collaboration, but the available evidence is not broad enough to claim every task is impossible to automate. It does show why a narrow research prototype cannot justify promising to replace professional judgment. [03-c2][03-c5]

A responsible app could distinguish the user’s intention from the system’s suggestion, show alternatives, and ask for missing information. Any output used in a real theatre would need separate professional review. These are design principles inferred from the sources; their usability and educational effect remain untested. [03-c4]

**Visual suggestion:** Show a user editing a suggestion and a clear “review with your team” step.

## What should be tested next?

**Narration:** Future studies should ask whether beginners can explain design choices better after using the tool, revise an idea when the story or space changes, and recognize when a suggestion does not fit. Tests should include different stage layouts and repeated revisions—not just one polished demo. The sources collected so far do not provide those results. [03-c5]

The most credible first promise is modest: help newcomers ask better questions about story, space, focus, and change. Offer suggestions, make assumptions visible, and leave room for human judgment. That is a useful beginning for an app—and a more honest one.

**Visual suggestion:** End with the words “Explore. Compare. Revise.” over a stage sketch.
