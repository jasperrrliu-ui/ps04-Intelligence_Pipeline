# A Beginner’s Guide to Reading a Stage

## Opening

**Narration:** You do not need to be a stage designer to understand how a show directs your attention. A few practical questions can help turn “I liked the lighting” into a clearer observation. Today, we’ll look at how to notice story, space, performers, and evidence. [02-c1][02-c2]

**Visual suggestion:** Show a stage before a scene begins, then overlay the questions “Where are we?” and “Where does your eye go?”

## First, connect the story to the room

**Narration:** Scenic designer Andrew Cohen describes a process that includes reading the script, talking with the director, and adapting ideas to the theatre. Stage layouts and rigging options shape what can be placed, moved, or seen. A design has to fit both the play and the room. [02-c1]

As a viewer, try this: write down what the scene seems to need the audience to understand. Then notice where performers enter, where they move, and which parts of the space are used. You are not guessing a single correct design. You are practicing how to connect a story moment to a physical choice.

**Visual suggestion:** Draw a basic stage rectangle and mark an entrance, an actor path, and one important object.

## Watch what light makes noticeable

**Narration:** Lighting designer Bruno Poet frames the work around what the audience should see and feel. Light can focus attention or create atmosphere; darkness can also be a deliberate choice. The goal is not to show every available effect. It is to select what serves the moment. [02-c2]

During a show, choose one scene and track where your attention goes. Does a light change redirect you? Does darkness create a pause or conceal a transition? These are small observations, but they help explain what the design is doing instead of relying only on taste.

**Visual suggestion:** Show three still stage compositions with different areas illuminated. Label them “focus,” “atmosphere,” and “darkness,” as examples rather than rules.

## Check what a source actually proves

**Narration:** The AuraLuxMuse paper describes research into generating lighting cues in relation to music and preferences. It also names challenges: how to represent lighting choices, align music and cues, incorporate preferences, and evaluate results. This is evidence of a research direction and reported experiments—not proof that the system can manage a complete production or improve every designer’s workflow. [02-c3][02-c4]

The same care applies to interviews. One designer’s account can show how that person approaches a problem; it does not automatically establish a universal rule. Ask who is making the claim, what supports it, and what has not been tested.

**Visual suggestion:** Put a claim on screen, then separate “source says” from “not yet established.”

## Take a four-part viewing note

**Narration:** After a show, write down four things: the scene’s location, the performers’ paths, the moment your attention shifts, and how the scene changes. Add one question: what did you see or hear that led to your interpretation? This is a project learning suggestion, not a method whose educational effect has been tested. [02-c6]

Different venues also change the design problem. Cohen’s account and a Boulder Ensemble Theater Company interview describe work shaped by the spaces where it has to function. Those examples remind us to consider conditions before judging a choice. They do not prove that every design task is beyond automation. [02-c5]

**Visual suggestion:** Show a short viewing-note template with four blank fields and one “What evidence did I notice?” prompt.

## Closing

**Narration:** The sources in this project are interviews, production examples, and a research paper. They offer useful perspectives, but they do not provide a large study of beginner learning or long-term AI use. Start by noticing how story, bodies, space, light, and transitions relate. That habit will help you question AI suggestions instead of accepting them as answers. [02-c6]

**Visual suggestion:** End on the viewing-note template, then fade to a live stage.
