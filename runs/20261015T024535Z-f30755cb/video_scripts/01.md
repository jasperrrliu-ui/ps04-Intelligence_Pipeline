# How Stage Designers Turn Stories into Space and Light

## Opening

**Narration:** A stage image can feel effortless when we watch a show. Behind it are decisions about story, bodies, space, and attention. Let’s look at how scenic and lighting designers shape those decisions—and what a beginner can learn by watching. [01-c1][01-c2]

**Visual suggestion:** Open on a wide view of an empty stage, then show a simple lighting change that shifts attention to one area.

## The live audience shares one space

**Narration:** In film, a camera can move closer or cut to a new view. In theatre, the audience watches the same physical space together. Lighting and sound help guide attention and keep a scene moving. Walter Trarbach describes this as part of the live experience: the audience’s response can affect the energy onstage in real time. [01-c1]

**Visual suggestion:** Compare a framed film image with a wide stage view. Highlight how different audience members can look at different parts of the same stage.

## Choices depend on story and venue

**Narration:** More lighting effects do not automatically make a stronger design. Bruno Poet emphasizes the lasting questions: What should the audience see and feel? Where should light come from? When is darkness more useful? The answers depend on the story and the room. [01-c2]

Scenery also changes with the theatre. Andrew Cohen discusses layouts and rigging systems that affect what can be moved or hung. His point is practical: a design must fit the play and the space, while keeping attention on the actors. A National Theatre feature about *The Bacchae* schools tour gives one example of adapting a scenic idea across spaces. [01-c3][01-c4]

**Visual suggestion:** Use a simple top-down stage diagram. Mark actor paths, audience sightlines, and one possible scenic transition.

## What AI research can tell us

**Narration:** The AuraLuxMuse research paper describes a prototype that attempts to generate lighting cues in relation to music and design preferences. The authors report results from their evaluation settings. They also identify challenges in representing lighting choices, aligning music and cues, incorporating preferences, and measuring quality. These results do not prove the system can design for every venue or run a full production. [01-c5][01-c6]

## A beginner’s exercise

**Narration:** Next time you watch a scene, note where the action happens, how performers move, where the light sends your attention, and what changes between moments. Ask what story information those choices reveal. This is a learning exercise suggested by the sources, not a tested curriculum. [01-c8]

Stage design joins artistic intention to physical conditions. AI may support bounded experiments, but beginners can start by learning to notice how story, bodies, space, and audience attention work together.

**Visual suggestion:** End with a four-question viewing card: space, movement, focus, transition.
