# What a Beginner-Facing AI Stage-Design App Should—and Should Not—Promise

An app that helps someone imagine a stage from a text prompt is an appealing idea. But the sources gathered for this project describe a broader professional practice: designers interpret stories, adapt ideas to venues, collaborate with other people, and adjust choices through rehearsal. A useful product should make those steps easier to explore while being honest about what current evidence can support. [03-c1][03-c2][03-c3][03-c4][03-c5]

## The product opportunity is guided exploration

Professional lighting is not simply a choice of color or brightness. Designers use light to guide attention, shape atmosphere, and define a story space. Walter Trarbach and Bruno Poet discuss how lighting works with sound, story, and the audience’s experience. Poet also emphasizes the value of darkness and of choosing where the audience should look. These interviews offer design perspectives; they are not controlled evaluations of an app. [03-c1]

For a beginner, the first product goal could be to make hidden questions visible. When a user enters a short scene description, the app might ask: Where does the scene take place? Which character or object should attract attention? What changes during the scene? What should remain unseen? These are proposed product prompts, not capabilities demonstrated by the collected sources. Their learning value would need to be tested with users. [03-c4]

## Treat space as an input, not decoration

Scenic designer Andrew Cohen describes designing for both the play and the particular theatre. Stage layout and rigging options affect what can be placed overhead, how scenery can move, and how actors remain visible. Those constraints mean a text prompt alone cannot fully specify a workable stage. [03-c2]

An early app could help users describe a simplified stage plan and connect their choices to the story: where actors enter, where a major object sits, and which area should draw focus. It could then present editable alternatives and explain their trade-offs in plain language. This is a product hypothesis based on the interviews, not a proven workflow or a substitute for technical plans, structural checks, or production collaboration.

## What the AI research supports

The AuraLuxMuse paper describes a prototype for generating lighting cues related to music and design preferences. Its authors report experiments and discuss limitations involving structured lighting parameters, multimodal alignment, personalization, and evaluation. The paper is relevant to the possibility of AI-assisted lighting, but it does not establish general performance across real theatres or show that the system can interpret any written script as a complete scenic design. [03-c3]

That boundary should shape the product language. The app could offer a few visual directions, explain which user choices shaped them, and invite revision. It should present an output as a starting point to discuss, not as an expert-approved design. Whether the suggestions help beginners learn or simply make attractive images is an empirical question. [03-c4]

## Keep human decisions and uncertainty in the loop

Designers work with performers, directors, technicians, and the practical conditions of a venue. Interviews describe those collaborations and adaptations, but the current evidence is not broad enough to conclude that every part of the work is impossible to automate. It does show why a product should not claim to replace professional judgment based on a narrow research prototype. [03-c2][03-c5]

The app should make uncertainty legible. It can distinguish a user’s stated intention from the system’s suggestion, show alternative interpretations, and ask for feedback when spatial details are missing. Any technical output intended for a real theatre would require separate professional review. These are responsible design principles inferred from the sources; their usability and educational effect remain to be tested. [03-c4]

## What should be evaluated next

Future evaluation should ask whether beginners can explain their design choices better after using the tool, whether they can revise a suggestion when the story or space changes, and whether professionals find the outputs useful as conversation starters. Tests should include varied stage layouts and repeated revisions, not only a single polished demonstration. The collected sources do not yet provide this evidence. [03-c5]

The most credible first version is therefore a learning scaffold: it helps a newcomer ask better questions about story, space, focus, and change. It may offer visual and lighting suggestions, but it should leave interpretation open, explain its assumptions, and invite human revision. That promise is smaller than “AI designs the show,” and more useful for someone beginning to understand how stage design works.
