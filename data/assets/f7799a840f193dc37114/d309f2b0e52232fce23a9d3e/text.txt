The ‘Pilgrimage’ Gremlin
Travel is fatal to prejudice - Mark Twain
KARIMAHSep 27, 20262ShareIn a world where almost everything can come to us, why do we still feel called to go somewhere else?
Hey gremlins. I’m in China on a group residency. It’s Sunday morning and I’m sitting in the quietest place I can find, which happens to be the children’s play area attached to the hotel breakfast buffet. I’m looking out at a skyline of stacked concrete buildings, their reflections cast into the lake below. It’s extra humid today. The mist will lift eventually and the smog will sit in the sky. Every now and then a cleaning robot comes out of the elevator, does a lap of the floor and disappears again. The music moves inexplicably between elevator jazz, Chinese karaoke and the sort of spiritual house music you might hear at a Burning Man afterparty. Bizarre. Although perhaps the more bizarre thing is that I have travelled thousands of miles across the world to seek refuge in a plastic children’s play den to write this.
Because why do we travel, after all?
It’s something I’ve been thinking about a lot while I’ve been here. Not just holidays or seeing somewhere new, but the older impulse to leave home in search of something that cannot be found from where you are. Artists have always travelled. People go on pilgrimages. We cross mountains and oceans, walk ancient routes, disappear into monasteries, move cities. We leave and we return.
What is strange now is that technically, almost everything can come to us. Before coming here I could research China from my studio in London. I could read about Mazu, the sea goddess. I could study the Maritime Silk Road, look at paintings, zoom into maps, read poetry, watch films, order books and scroll through thousands of images of Fujian without leaving my sofa.
But knowing about a place is not the same as entering it.
You can’t research humidity. Or how a landscape changes when you’ve been looking at it through a bus window for three hours. And some things you wouldn’t choose at all; standing in a room where you can’t understand or read a thing, or waking at 4am because your body still thinks it’s somewhere else. There is knowledge that seems to require a body, and perhaps that is part of why we still go.
A new environment also gives you a strange permission to become someone else. Although I don’t think “reinvention” is quite the right word. It feels more like a permission slip for a part of yourself that doesn’t usually get to come online. And in return, you have to offer something of yourself too. Through sharing, teaching, being generous, or sometimes sacrificing an old belief to make room for a new one.
I’m finding this particular journey difficult to write about while I’m still inside it. There are good things and bad, and it’s too soon to be so on the nose about what those are. But there is a pattern I’ve noticed, both internally and externally, and it is one of refinement.
And so I bring it back to the personal. Maybe it’s age, maybe it’s the speed at which everything around us is changing, but my tolerance for what feels extraneous is getting borderline unhinged. Old metrics of success and visibility feel increasingly unwell. Even here, surrounded by extraordinary technology, automation and robots gliding around the hotel, I keep wondering what all this advancement is actually advancing us towards.
And so I bring it back to the personal. For me, and maybe you too, something about the nature of movement is changing.
I don’t feel particularly interested in travelling simply to be excited anymore. Maybe I’m at a different point in my life, and after years of expansion I’m interested in contraction. Not contraction as making my life smaller, but concentrating it. Taking all of these scattered particles and asking what happens when I stay still long enough to build something with them.
Because I used to think movement created momentum. Now I’m wondering whether sometimes momentum requires staying put.
Which is an inconvenient revelation to have while sitting several thousand miles away from home.
But perhaps that’s exactly what pilgrimage has always contained: the return. You leave because something is waiting for you elsewhere, but you don’t necessarily belong there. You keep going. You encounter something, offer something of yourself in exchange, and come home carrying what remains.
