Museum Rat #53: Q&A with Sarah Crowner on Pastoral, Barbican
Ahead of the UK premiere I spoke to painter Sarah Crowner about what it looks like to put your own studio on a stage
KARIMAHSep 25, 20261Share
Pastoral | Barbican Theatre
1 to 3 October 2026
Credit Lawrence Sumulong
The gist: The gist: a dance work built on Beethoven’s Sixth, with a set by the painter Sarah Crowner and a lighting design that reenacts the path of sunlight across her Brooklyn studio floor.
Credit Maria Baranova
When I call Sarah Crowner, it’s early morning in Brooklyn and late afternoon in London, and the first thing we talk about, before Pastoral, before Beethoven, before any of it, is the weather. London has turned crisp and autumnal; New York, she tells me, is still summery, and in no hurry to let the season go. It’s a small exchange, but it sets the tone for everything that follows: two artists comparing notes on light, air, and the particular quality of a place, before either of us says the word “art.”
Crowner is a painter, first and always, known for canvases she cuts apart and stitches back together. Hard-edged fields of jewel-toned green, red and yellow carry within them the memory of having once been whole, then undone, then made anew. For Pastoral, the dance work she has made with the choreographer Pam Tanowitz and the composer Caroline Shaw, that language of cutting and rebuilding has migrated off the wall entirely: into curtains that move with dancers, into a stretched canvas that unrolls across a stage, into a design that behaves less like scenery and more like weather.
Left, Pastoral Credit Lawrence Sumulong, Right Maria Baranova
The piece, a Barbican co-commission, premiered at Bard College’s Fisher Center last summer and arrives at the Barbican Theatre for its UK premiere on 1 October. At its heart is Beethoven’s Symphony No. 6, the Pastoral, though what that means in practice is something more personal than a simple restaging of a classical theme.
“It started with conversations I had with Pam about where she was, creatively, at that particular moment,” Crowner tells me. Tanowitz wanted a dance set to the Beethoven. The two of them began turning the word itself over. What does pastoral even mean? “As artists, we think of a pastoral as an idyllic nature setting, rolling hills, peace, utopia. We were just throwing out words as we brainstormed.”
Credit Maria Baranova
“Nothing is original, and everything comes from somewhere else. What’s original, maybe, is how these different things work together.”
But the image that actually seeded the piece, Crowner says, was not a historical one. It was her own daily walk into her Red Hook studio, through a courtyard sometimes overrun with weeds and willow trees, and into a room flooded with skylight. “I always kind of have the feeling of nature, whether it’s through light or through certain botanical forms,” she says. “In my head, as you start to work.” She had been making a series of large green paintings around the time she and Tanowitz began talking. When Tanowitz visited the studio, they began to wonder: what if those paintings could grow beyond the canvas and become something a body might move through, rather than simply stand in front of?
That thought experiment is now, more or less, the stage design for Pastoral. Enlarged canvases arre embodied through dance. A red painting and a yellow one, kept at Crowner’s usual six-by-six-foot scale, appear in one sequence beneath a lighting design, by Davison Scandrett, built to replicate the exact path of sunlight moving across Crowner’s studio wall and floor over the course of a day. “If you lie around my studio all day, you’ll see the squares of light move throughout the day, because the position of the sun moves,” she says. Scandrett found a way to do that with stage light. “The stage design moves from images of nature, images of painting, to an actual reenactment of the path of light.”
Credit Maria Baranova
It is, when you sit with it, a strange and lovely thing to have done: to take the most private, unwitnessed hours of a working life, the hours spent alone in a studio watching light cross a floor, and stage them, publicly, as choreography. I tell Crowner that I recognise the feeling she is describing, the way a moving shadow can make you suddenly, acutely aware of time passing, of the odd proportion between a human body and the duration of a day. It reframes the question I had come to the call with, about how a painting becomes something a dancer moves through. The answer, it turns out, is not about translating an image. It is about translating an experience of duration.
Pastoral emerged through conversation: between Crowner, Tanowitz and Shaw, and between their distinct ways of working. “The process of making it was a conversation, between me, Pam and Caroline,” Crowner says, “back and forth through several months, on Zoom, on the phone, over coffee, in studio visits.” Shaw’s score carries that exchange further, weaving scratchy early-twentieth-century recordings of Beethoven’s Pastoral Symphony with birdsong, insects, dishes being washed and a car honking past an open window. History, nature and the city begin to occupy the same soundscape. “That’s kind of what Pam does too, and what I do,” Crowner says. “I’m thinking about the physical space where I live. I’ve got nature, I’ve got city, and in the studio I’m always grappling with the history of art, and where our work fits into that whole long conversation.”
Left Credit Maria Baranova Right Lawrence Sumulong
“There is a reward that comes from slow looking and slow listening. I just hope people will slow down and appreciate.”
That question, of how to make work this steeped in history feel alive rather than reverent, seems to me the real subject of Pastoral, whether or not anyone involved would put it that way. Beethoven, after all, is about as loaded a historical reference as a contemporary artist can reach for, and painting, Crowner’s own medium, carries its own centuries of weight. Her answer, when I ask her directly, is disarmingly practical. “I think, as artists, if we can let go of the pressure to create something brand new and original every time we walk into the studio, that helps,” she says. “Nothing is original, and everything comes from somewhere else. What’s original, maybe, is how these different things work together, how you draw elements from the past and make them new again. It’s always easier to start with something that exists than from a completely clean slate.”
What she wants from an audience, in the end, is not comprehension of any of this scaffolding but simply attention. “I hope people slow down,” she says. “Everything moves so fast now, we’re all embedded in our phones. With a performance like this, you’re sitting for an hour. You don’t have access to your phone. All you have is your eyes, your ears, your body. There is a reward that comes from slow looking and slow listening.”
Credit Lawrence Sumulong
As for how a work built from Beethoven, New York light and American land will read in London, Crowner is candidly unsure, and interested in finding out. “You have this tradition there, of the pastoral, of British landscape painting, Constable, Turner,” she says. “In America, or at least the East Coast, the landscape is a lot wilder, more untamed. I always think of the English landscape as more curated.” It is, as it happens, exactly the kind of institution built to hold that tension: the Barbican, a Brutalist fortress with strong, bold opinions of its own, and an audience fluent enough in art history to catch every reference Crowner, Tanowitz and Shaw have folded in, while also expecting something new. Crowner, when I put this to her, simply says she hopes it works. She hopes it looks good.
By the time we hang up, I have the sense of an artist still, in some sense, standing in her own courtyard: aware of the weeds and the willow trees, aware of the ugly ten-minute walk through the city that precedes them, knowing that both belong equally to the work. Pastoral opens at the Barbican Theatre on 1 October. Crowner arrives in London on the 30th, with the design, she tells me, entirely locked in. For the first time in months, she says, she gets to simply watch. “All the prep has been done,” she says. “You just get to be present, which is really nice.”
Museum Rat verdict?
I can't wait to go and see this. As a painter it's going to be interesting to see how the music, costume and set come together. Three nights and then it's gone, so definitely a watch.

Planning Your Visit
What: Pastoral, choreographed by Pam Tanowitz, décor by Sarah Crowner, music by Caroline Shaw, lighting by Davison Scandrett. Seven dancers, three live musicians.
Where: Barbican Theatre, Silk Street, London EC2Y 8DS.
When: 1 to 3 October 2026. One hour, no interval. Age 10+.
Tickets: from £22. Young Barbican, 16 to 29, £12. Under 16 from £13. Members and groups of ten or more get 20% off.
Access: audio-described performance on Friday 2 October at 7.45pm, with a pre-show touch tour.
Worth knowing: it premiered at the Fisher Center at Bard College in June 2025. This is its UK premiere, co-commissioned by the Barbican.
