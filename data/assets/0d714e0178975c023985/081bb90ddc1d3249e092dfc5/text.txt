Grace and Graciousness: Nick Mullikin’s Giselle (Part II: The Result)
Y. Kendall |
October 12, 2026
For Part I, click here.
The audience vibrated with eagerness. It was clear from nearby comments that people were excited for him, clapping exuberantly when he walked on stage. After all, it was his night—his night— the first public performance of his first full-length choreography, Giselle, a canonical work that premiered in Paris 185 years ago.
But then, Nick Mullikin, artistic director of the Nashville Ballet, performed an act of unmitigated grace. He shared his night with the musicians of the Nashville Symphony. Once they realized just what they were witnessing, the audience stood up, giving the Symphony members a vigorous, and lengthy, standing ovation before they had played a single note of Adolphe Adam’s famed score. These professionals, at that time furloughed from a canceled season, were able to play because NB has funding independent of the Symphony’s beleaguered budget.
Under the expert baton of Ming Luke, the Symphony repaid the audience tribute with some of the most sublime playing I’ve heard from them in some time. The ending, with the white-clad spirit disappearing into the mist to the solo flute melody (Erik Gratton) delicately accompanied by solo harp (Licia Jaskunas) was absolutely exquisite. Staging a mysterious stranger with flute and harp at both opening and ending framed this tale thoughtfully.
Before that, Mullikin showed his firm understanding of classical ballet technique, as the dancers showed their growth toward the technical perfection developed as part of the tradition for such iconic works. For those who don’t know the story:
Act I: Village maiden Giselle loves to dance, but has a heart problem. Village gamekeeper Hilarion loves her, but she only has eyes for Loys, the newcomer. Loys, though, is actually Duke Albrecht in disguise. He is already engaged to Bathilde who comes near the village with a noble hunting party after Hilarion has discovered Albrecht’s treachery. In despair, Giselle dances, then dies.
Jamie Kopit as Giselle and Garritt McCabe as Loys/Albrecht (photo: Karyn Photography)
As intermission began, an audience member exclaimed, “The main character just died and we’re halfway through!?! I am so confused.”
Act II: Once dead, Giselle’s spirit is invited to join the ranks of the Wilis, prematurely deceased young maidens who had all been jilted at the altar or otherwise betrayed. All are dressed in filmy wedding white. Led by Queen Myrtha, they hunt and kill men found in the woods at night. They had already found Hilarion weeping at Giselle’s tomb. Later, they find Albrecht at the tomb. In a departure from the original plot, where Giselle’s pleas save him from the Wilis, in Mullikin’s version, Albrecht also dies.
Throughout, there is attractive work by the corps de ballet. This is especially the case with the Wilis of Act II. The virtuosity of the troupe is shown in arabesques (straight standing leg, other raised straight back at a 90º angle). The balance and core strength is notable, yet maintaining this position while hopping forward, which the audience recognized as impressive, seemed slightly awkward. There was also an awkward moment when a bench was brought out, apparently for the sole purpose of serving as a platform to pose Giselle.
More natural was the use of folk dance patterns—circles, heys (interweaving lines), and skipping for the villagers, representing both rustic life and youth. The plethora of duets and quartets for smaller villager ensembles indicated a worthy desire to feature more of these talented dancers, but the extended length and number of these features overbalanced the narrative flow. Unlike Nutcracker, this ballet doesn’t have the variety of characters to justify so many features.
(Photo: Karyn Photography)
Also natural, though, and a fitting homage to the past, the pantomimic gestures that advance the story scenes (ex. Loys tapping his head when he forgets something), took us back to the roots of classical French ballet in eighteenth-century ballet-pantomimes by Baroque composers like Jean-Philippe Rameau where humor, magic, and romance coexisted in harmony. However, Giselle, a village maiden, physically laying hands on members of the nobility did not ring true to the realities of class status.
Soloists Jamie Kopit (Giselle), Garritt McCabe (Albrecht), and Nicholas Mihlar (Hilarion) did fine jobs. Sarah Pierce, as Bathilde danced well, but her costume’s limited circumference seemed restrictive, somehow lacking elegance. One other costume issue: it would be nice to have more distinction between Giselle and the other Wilis.
The real standout in dancing was Summer Montenegro as Myrtha, Queen of the Wilis. The combination of beautiful choreography and beautiful technique was a wonderful match. In fact, one of the most compelling scenes highlighted Hilarion dancing with the Wilis. Mihlar’s bold and commanding leaps certainly resonated as a complement to the Wilis swirling delicacy.
Bravo to Maestro Mullikin for an excellent debut as choreographer of classic ballet and, likewise, thanks for this vital reminder of the importance of both the Nashville Ballet and Symphony to the arts of the Music City.

Y Kendall
Y Kendall is a Stanford-educated musicologist, specializing in dance history who recently earned an MFA in Creative Nonfiction at Columbia University, studying nonfiction writing with Ben Ratliff and Margo Jefferson. Kendall’s diverse works have been published in Alchemy: Journal of Translation, Columbia Journal, Mitos Magazín, The Hunger Mountain Review, and The Salt Collective, among others. Born and raised in Tennessee, Kendall now lives near Nashville, freelancing as a flutist and writer, while caregiving for relatives.
