The ‘White Cube’ Gremlin
Quit clutching your pearls. The future belongs to Gremlins.
KARIMAHSep 20, 20261Share
Where does art belong?
Dear Gremlins, well dressed and otherwise.
I write to you from a bellowing underground carriage, wearing Italian loafers that make me feel like Al Pacino in The Godfather, a white poplin shirt buttoned to the top that’s giving Willem Dafoe, and lace shorts that look like pyjamas. Several references, one outfit. And yet, of course, it works. I like what happens when you borrow something from one world and put it in another. Each piece brings its associations, but the new company changes how you read it.
And to me, this is what art is; lending symbolism from one place and taking it into another.
This week I spoke at W1 Curates in Ibiza, at Hï nightclub, as part of Culture Collective, a weekend bringing art from around the world to the island. Yes, Ibiza, where people pop pills and dance on squelchy floors till 5am. It’s also a place where dance music mixes with soul and stories of Atlantis are born. At Hï, I was talking about art, women’s role in culture and race politics while my paintings filled the screens behind me.
People sometimes struggle with the border between fine art in galleries and art that enters public life. But watching people encounter my work in that room, I wasn’t thinking about a border. I was thinking about what becomes possible when art meets people where they are. I can find the divine on a prayer mat and at the DJ decks. Why should art have only one place to belong?
Giving talks in spaces like this brings me back to the same principle, time and time again:
Art belongs to everyone.
Food and music move easily between worlds. Painting can too, but its visceral power is often kept behind the doors of a white cube that many people feel intimidated to enter. I love the ritual of museums. I also love the idea that a gallery of the future might appear on a screen at Oxford Circus, in a repurposed clothes shop, or behind the DJ decks. Putting art in those places lets more people encounter it.
But being able to see art is only the beginning. Who feels welcome to have an opinion about it? I took notes on this in the nightclub, because who else would but a Gremlin?
Michael Burgess, a creative producer working across culture put it plainly: “yes, we want to sell art, but I want to make sure there is meaning, purpose and consciousness in this function.” As new audiences enter the art world, the question goes beyond where we put the work. How do we make people feel that the conversation belongs to them, too?
Ulrich Schrauth, curator and creative director, came at it from a different angle. Museums come to him and say “we want to diversify our audiences, can we do something digital” and his answer is always the same. “You have to think from the ground up how to represent these voices and how to interweave them.”
So what do cultural spaces need for the next generation? Collaboration, for one. Places where people can gather, make memories and feel safe enough to speak. They also need urgency. We’re living through disruptive times, and art should make room for political conversation and action, not just reflect on them years later.
And they need stories. Whose roots shaped the work? Who was it made for? How did it arrive in this room? Those questions can give someone a way in, especially when the language around art makes them feel they need permission to speak.
“The gallery world is, kind of, crumbling” Ian Davenport, the blue chip artist, said “lots of artists are taking this time to pause their practice and review it.”
W1 saw that gallery spaces weren’t keeping up with how people experience art, and they built the tech before anyone else was thinking that way. Nobody took them seriously at first, but now their screens are built for purpose. So if galleries are struggling, the real question isn’t digital versus physical, it’s how do you support artists in a way that uses both?
This makes me hopeful. Not that the barriers and gatekeepers to the art world don’t exist. Trust me, they do. Money, gender, race, location: there are many ways to be kept outside. But these new spaces open a different door.
Someone who would never plan a gallery visit might find themselves standing inside a painting on a night out. My great swathes of colour, which usually live quietly on a single canvas, suddenly surrounded the room. The work hadn’t changed, but its invitation had. I could take people into my world rather than wait for them to find their way to it. Which is why, when W1 asked, I thought: sure, let’s see what happens. What have I got to lose?
As for you. What about putting your music/food/tarot into a cinema screening, or a warehouse drum and base set, who knows? All I know is that these worlds are mixing. Right now everything is up for grabs, so rather than clutch your pearls, be like the dandy dressing folks on the tube and mix it up. Lend from symbols of culture in one place to create your own eclectic style in another.
The future belongs to the Gremlins after all.
