MCR at the Movies:
The 2026 Nashville Film Festival
Grace Krenz |
October 8, 2026
This September the Nashville Film Festival celebrated its 57th year! At a variety of venues spread across Nashville, the festival screens films, holds a creators conference, and hosts parties, mixers, and happy hours.
Once again, we at MCR were delighted to get to cover it. With 150 films available, giving complete coverage to the annual event is always an impossible task, but we have a great time trying. Our writers covered narrative features, documentary features, several shorts collections, and several awards winners. Here’s a look at our experience at the festival this year:
Winner of Best Music Documentary Feature: Jail Time Records
I love going blind into films. Many of my favorite and most cherished viewing experiences have come from deciding to watch a film off nothing more than the basis of initial intrigue. When I was combing through the repertoire that the 2026 Nashville Film Festival was to feature, I was struck by the log line of one of the music documentary features: a film documenting the first recording studio inside an African prison. With my interest piqued, I attended the screening of Jail Time Records. And I am so very glad that I did.
Jail Time Records is a documentary directed by Italian filmmaker Dione Roach and Cameroonian music producer Steve Happi. The film derives its name from the non-profit record label that was founded inside New Bell Prison in Douala, Cameroon. In 2018, Steve Happi, then incarcerated at New Bell, helped spearhead the construction of a recording studio, making Jail Time Records the first studio inside an African prison. The film was shot over the course of six years and documents men incarcerated inside New Bell, loosely following three artists signed to the label: Empereur, Stone Larabik, and Transporteur.
A very honed artistic cohesion ties together both the documentary and the output of the Jail Time Records label. Beyond directing the documentary feature, Dione Roach is credited with directing many of the label’s music videos. Likewise, Steve Happi created the music used for the documentary, also producing most all of the tracks released on the Jail Time label. This creates a sense of aesthetic continuity among the documentary’s staged and unstaged elements.
Jail Time Records is undoubtedly one of the most visually impressive releases of the year. In an introductory sequence, handheld camerawork captures the absolute claustrophobia inside New Bell, a prison listed at a capacity of 800 inmates that houses over 6,000. Viewers weave through narrow alleyways packed with people, eavesdrop tense altercations and witness playful exchanges, guided by the charismatic La PJ. The color grading heightens already impressive images, and the manipulation of natural light and shadows is very striking. My mind goes to a moment where Empereur shows viewers the solitary confinement room, demonstrating how guards would chain inmates, suspending them in air by an arm and a leg, to press them for information. He silently assumes the pose on the ground and holds, sculpture-like in the dark room, sunlight creeping in through the openings in the doorframe.
The editing of Jail Time Records is also a tremendous strong suit of the film. Throughout the documentary, Roach and Happi establish a loose form that switches between more narrative-oriented segments, focused on the men, capturing conversation and interaction, and more artistic, prepared shots of inmates posing, akin to what one would find in a book of a photographer’s work. Many of these expository segments are supported with music from the label. The visuals frequently fall into step with the songs, and the edit of the film possesses a palpable musical quality. Roach and Happi were able to capture and document many of the recording sessions that turned into studio releases, and several times throughout the documentary, they brilliantly transition from this fly-on-the-wall perspective in the studio into the fully produced music video for the completed song.
More straight-ahead portions of the film serve to document events that unfold over time. Through Transporteur, we see him await a sentencing, possibly facing capital punishment. Eventually, the day of his sentencing comes and he receives a very favorable verdict, permitting his release within months. The end of the film shows Transporteur donning an all-white outfit, elated as struts through New Bell, now a bona-fide celebrity among his fellow inmates from his music. Before he leaves, he provides words of encouragement to Empereur, reminding him that Empereur’s time will come as well. As he exits, he is cheered on by an incoming bus of inmates headed towards the prison. Witnessing Transporteur effectively walk on water after standing in the face of execution was an ecstatic moment, and one couldn’t help but share in the joy of his release.
Thematically, Jail Time Records proves to be just as rich, showcasing tremendous depth in the lives of the artists they feature. Creative expression is one of the few methods in which men can stake their individuality. There is a recurring visual motif of wings and flight that beautifully reinforces the idea of freedom, of rebirth, of redemption and overcoming obstacles. Several shots linger on birds flying overhead above the walls of New Bell. Towards the end of the film, a man is shown holding angel wings by his side while standing atop a roof. For the men signed to Jail Time, art is a tool for liberation, and music is their wings.
Jail Time Records strikes a very respectful balance of advocating for the humanity of the men inside New Bell while also being candid in including the burdens and consequences of their past decisions. There is a particularly moving sequence where Larabik Stone calls his young daughter on the phone. He shares that he has been working on new music, and asks if she can sing for him. We listen as his daughter happily sings a song for Stone as he begins to cry. It is a wonderfully innocent and tender moment, showing that music serves as a connective tissue that supersedes the physical barriers that New Bell imposes. The aforementioned sequence with Larabik Stone is juxtaposed with a segment where he shares about his time as a soldier where he committed very serious acts of violence against people. Larabik cited those decisions during that period as his greatest regrets. As a viewer, it is difficult to contend with this knowledge when so much of the film captures the firm kindness and even-keeled nature of Stone, as well as his creative output with Jail Time.
Jail Time Records is a stunning collage of the creative efforts of the Jail Time label and of the day-to-day experiences of the men living within New Bell prison. It reminds viewers of the transformative power of artistic expression and shows that access to creative environments can produce some truly stellar art, even in the most inhospitable conditions. I am so glad to have seen this and eagerly await upcoming work from Happi, Roach, and the rest of the Jail Time label.
-EM
Crooks: A Little Noir, a Little Grindhouse, and a Lot of Trouble
There is something refreshing about a film that knows exactly what it wants to be. Crooks, Mickey Keating’s compact, stylish crime thriller, does not pretend to reinvent the language of film noir. Instead, it embraces that language with the enthusiasm of a filmmaker who has spent a great deal of time watching movies—and then proceeds to have some fun with what he has learned.
At only 76 minutes, Crooks moves with the efficiency of a good pulp novel. There is little time for hesitation. Faye (Angela Trimbur), a nightclub singer with a criminal past, finds herself in need of money. When Johnny (Chase Williamson), an old associate, offers her the opportunity to participate in a robbery, she takes the bait. The robbery goes badly, Faye takes the money and runs, and before long she is being pursued by a professional killer known as the Fixer (Keith Kupferer). Her attempt to escape eventually leads her to an isolated diner, where she encounters Blanche (Melora Walters), the seemingly ordinary waitress who turns out to be considerably more complicated than she first appears. It really is very noir.
But Crooks is less interested in telling a new story than in seeing how many familiar stories it can fit into those 76 minutes. Keating’s film draws openly from the visual and narrative vocabulary of classic noir, pulp fiction, grindhouse cinema, and the kind of off-kilter crime movies that populated repertory theaters and video stores for decades. The result is deliberately artificial, a cinematic Chicago that is a place of shadowy streets, neon, gangsters, diners, crooked cops, desperate criminals and people who seem to have wandered in from entirely different movies—this artificiality is one of the film’s pleasures.
The movie’s opening establishes the tone immediately. The imagery is rendered in stark black and white, giving Faye’s world the appearance of a noir photograph come to life. Thankfully, the aesthetic is stylized without being precious. Then, as Faye leaves the city and heads into the Midwest, the visual and generic world suddenly changes. Color enters the picture, and with it comes a different cinematic vocabulary. What begins as traditional noir gradually mutates into something closer to grindhouse pulp. One moment we seem to be watching a lost crime picture from the 1940s; the next, Keating has transported us into a movie that feels indebted to the more sinister, stranger cinema of the 1970s. In particular, the golden light of the sun in the later chapters are strikingly reminiscent of the Texas Chainsaw Massacre or The Devil’s Rejects, not to mention the gore.
Keating’s previous films have similarly demonstrated his fascination with genre and its history, and Crooks makes that fascination particularly visible. The danger is obvious: when a filmmaker wears his influences this openly, the movie can begin to feel like an exercise in imitation rather than a work with a personality of its own.
For stretches of Crooks, that danger is real. The plot is intentionally familiar. People double-cross one another. Somebody has stolen money. Somebody else wants it back. A seemingly simple robbery becomes increasingly complicated. The film depends upon the audience recognizing these conventions even as Keating rearranges them. There are echoes of the Coen brothers, Quentin Tarantino, classic noir and exploitation cinema, among other things. The references are not hidden, they are practically part of the production design.
Yet somewhere around the arrival at the diner, Crooks begins to glow and much of that has to do with Melora Walters.
As Blanche, Walters gives the movie its most unpredictable performance. At first, Blanche seems to be exactly what the situation requires: a lonely waitress in an isolated diner, someone who can provide Faye with shelter and perhaps a little temporary safety. But Walters gradually allows something more unsettling to emerge. Blanche is nervous, peculiar and increasingly difficult to read. She is also oddly strong. The performance walks a delicate line between vulnerability and menace.
Her scenes with Trimbur are the film’s real center. Faye and Blanche circle each other with the wary curiosity of two people who recognize something in one another but aren’t quite prepared to acknowledge it. There is humor in their exchanges, but there is also an underlying erotic and psychological tension. Both women are survivors. Both have secrets. Both understand that the person sitting across the table may be considerably more dangerous than she appears.
Trimbur is excellent as Faye precisely because she does not make the character into a conventional femme fatale. Faye is tired, angry and practical. She has made bad decisions, but she is not particularly interested in apologizing for them. Trimbur gives her enough vulnerability to make the danger surrounding her matter without ever making her seem helpless.
Last but not least, there is the Fixer. Kupferer’s killer belongs to a long tradition of nearly mythical movie assassins: the professional who enters a scene and changes its temperature simply by standing there. He is a force moving through the story without any development, which is appropriate for a film that is more interested in archetypes than realism.
And with the arrival of the Fixer into the sunshine, Crooks becomes something considerably stranger than its opening suggests. The final movement abandons much of the conventional noir atmosphere for a tense, almost surreal sequence that demonstrates just how far Keating is willing to push his premise. It is here that the film’s various influences finally stop feeling like references and begin to function as ingredients in Keating’s own cinematic language.
The limitation is also clear. Crooks is over almost as soon as it has established its world. At 76 minutes, there simply isn’t much room for the characters to develop beyond their archetypes. The twists are entertaining, but some of them feel more clever than consequential. One finishes the film admiring its craft while wishing Keating had another twenty minutes—or perhaps another hour—to explore the strange world he has created.
Crooks is not trying to be profound. It is trying to be stylish, nasty, funny and unpredictable. It succeeds often enough to make its shortcomings forgivable. At a time when so many films arrive burdened by the expectation that they must somehow explain their cultural importance, there is something liberating about a small independent movie that simply wants to entertain people who love movies Keating’s Crooks clearly loves movies.
It loves their conventions, their clichés, their shadows and their beautiful artificiality. More importantly, it understands that genre is not necessarily a prison. It might be derivative, but if you can embrace that, Keating’s world is your oyster.
At the Nashville Film Festival, Crooks felt like exactly the kind of discovery that makes a festival worthwhile: short, strange, unabashedly cinematic and just familiar enough to make you wonder where, exactly, you’ve seen it before. The answer, of course, is everywhere. The trick is what Keating does with all those pieces.
Mostly, he has fun, and for 76 minutes, so did I.
-JM
The Lorraine: A Sam Pollard Film
The Bar-Kays spent that night at the Lorraine Motel. Famous for their funky instrumentals like “Soul Finger” and “Son of Shaft,” they had rehearsed there, written songs there, ready to record at nearby Stax Records off Beale Street. But the night Martin Luther King was assassinated, the entire neighborhood was on lockdown. So they spent the night at the Lorraine Motel.
Documentary film director Sam Pollard has constructed a moving 84-minute tribute to the cultural and historical legacy of the Lorraine Motel. The owners, the residents, the employees, the activists, the curators, and the family were all included in chronicling this famed space from its past, as a location on the Green Book travelers guide for Black Americans, to its present as home to the National Civil Rights Museum (NCRM). Given its connections to the music business, it was right and proper that this showing on September 26, 2026 be held in the National Museum of African American Music.
The Lorraine had always been more than a safe haven for Black travelers in the Jim Crow era. In addition to housing and hosting meetings by Civil Rights activists like the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee, and the NAACP, owners Walter and Loree Bailey opened their swimming pool to local black kids, kids banned by law and practice from the public pools their parents’ taxes supported.
Touring musicians like the Staple Singers, Aretha Franklin, Booker T and the M.G.’s, and even Jazz great Lionel Hampton slept, partied, and collaborated there. But athletes stayed there as well, including the Harlem Globetrotters, Negro League players like Satchel Paige, and groundbreaking Dodger star Jackie Robinson. They stayed there until Martin Luther King, Jr. was killed.
Supported by executive producers Alvin D. Hall, Joe Wemple, Juleyka Lantigua, who also served as screenwriters, The Lorraine connects well-chosen historical footage, interviews with eyewitnesses, and commentary from those who currently work to maintain the Lorraine legacy. The fact that many of the eyewitnesses to the glory days and tragic initial demise of the Lorraine are still living says something to those who wish to see the Civil Rights era as ancient history.
For the most part, the difficult moments and controversies are faced in clear-eyed fashion. Coby Smith, a member of The Invaders, a Black Panthers-style community aide organization in Memphis, spoke of negotiating Sanitation Strike protest tactics with MLK’s team, but being blamed for violence instigated by police, and later being left out of the story told by the museum built around the Lorraine.
D’Army Bailey, founder of NCRM (a judge unrelated to the Lorraine owners) relives the herculean effort to raise money through his King Memorial Foundation when even Coretta Scott King initially opposed preserving the scene of her husband’s assassination. Bailey acknowledges the Sanitation Union—on whose behalf MLK had led protests—and their pledge of $140,000 to buy the motel. And one lone Memphian, Jacqueline Smith, has spent decades protesting money spent on a monument when the city closed public housing and businesses, leaving the citizens of a once-lively neighborhood destitute.
But some aspects of the story are left incomplete. Carolyn Bailey Champion, daughter, and Chandra Champion Walker, granddaughter of Lorraine owners Walter and Loree Bailey paint a loving picture of their relatives’ contributions to the community. But little is said of Loree’s repeated statement “What have we done?” uttered after the assassination, just days before she died on the same day as MLK’s funeral. Even less is said of Walter’s opening the Lorraine Motel to prostitutes and hustlers when the city leaders’ destruction of the neighborhood and federal desegregation of public places resulted in less financial viability for the Lorraine.
The pain of this history is clear in the tears of Mary Ellen Norwood Ford, (former Lorraine Motel housekeeper), and Noelle Trent (former director of the Civil Rights Museum’s History and Interpretation division). Both apologized for the same reaction. Ford apologized for “breaking down” even though her breakdown consisted of only a few tears that escaped her strength. Dressed in a black lacy cardigan over a dark orange dress, Trent similarly said, “I did not know I was going to cry.”
The continuing need for tears is reconfirmed by ending vignettes of more recent injustices: Colin Kaepernick; Trayvon Martin; George Floyd; the Tennessee 3 represented by Justin Pearson, state rep from Memphis; and even more recent “Abolish ICE” protests. The film reminds us that when the museum opened, rather than a ribbon, some chains were cut, and doves of peace were released.
In addition to Nashville, The Lorraine has been recognized by the Tribeca (NYC) and DC/DOX (Washington DC) film festivals.
-YK
Breeder: Breeding the Perfect Human
Alex Goyette’s Breeder is a horror movie while quietly asking a much older and more disturbing question: What gives human beings the right to decide which human beings should exist?
Certainly, eugenics has already given us its historical answer, and it is one of the ugliest chapters in modernity’s faith in science, classification, and improvement. But Breeder understands that the idea has not disappeared simply because the word makes us uncomfortable. It has merely learned to speak in different languages: optimization, genetic selection, exceptionalism, enhancement. Goyette takes that impulse and puts it in the hands of Patti, an eccentric poodle breeder played with magnificent menace by Dot-Marie Jones.
Patti’s great insight—or delusion—is wonderfully simple. If human beings are perfectly comfortable breeding dogs for particular characteristics, why should humans be exempt from the same logic? Why not breed for intelligence? Why not engineer the future by controlling who reproduces, and who they reproduce with? The horror, of course, is that the logic is so easy to understand.
Russell (Daniel Doheny), a young scientist desperate for funding for research intended to save an endangered bee population, becomes entangled in Patti’s scheme. His predicament gives the film an especially sharp edge because Breeder is not really interested in the familiar horror-movie question of whether someone will survive. It is interested in what someone will surrender in order to continue pursuing an ideal.
Money, as always, becomes the mechanism through which the moral principle is negotiated and humans objectified. The fact that scientific progress is something that can be purchased is laid bare. Human bodies become resources. Reproduction becomes a transaction. And the distance between “saving the bees” and “improving humanity” begins to look less substantial than Russell would like to believe, and there is the rub.
Goyette is playing with one of modernity’s most persistent fantasies: that nature is a problem to be solved. The film’s bees, poodles, and humans are not incidental details. They form a strange biological continuum. We breed animals. We manipulate environments. We select desirable traits. We call the result improvement. Patti simply extends the argument to its monstrously logical conclusion.
What makes the film particularly uncomfortable is its reversal of conventional gendered roles in the reproductive horror. Russell is a man being reduced to his reproductive capacity, quite literally a “stud” whose body becomes valuable primarily for what it can produce. (The term “stud” brings the breeding quickly back to the animal world) The reversal is deliberately provocative because it forces the audience to encounter sexual and reproductive coercion from a perspective that cinema has historically treated very differently.
Yet Goyette does not always know what to do with that reversal. The women surrounding Patti (her adopted daughters) can sometimes seem more like components of the film’s conceptual machinery than fully realized characters. Maddie Phillips’ (Gen V, The Boys) fetching character Ainsley feels morally complicated, but her ultimate position as protagonist is underdeveloped—it is treated as a reveal. In this way, the film’s provocative ideas occasionally outrun its emotional development. Breeder is at its most powerful when it trusts its central metaphor rather than explaining it.
Yet, then there is Jones. Patti is funny because she is horrifying, and horrifying because she is so matter-of-fact. Jones never plays her as a conventional monster. She is a physically imposing woman who is just as fearsome when she baby talks as when she commands. She plays Patti as someone who has simply decided that her worldview is rational—and that everyone else is irrational for refusing to accept it. That is considerably more frightening, and another way in which the Breeder’s currency fits the current world.
The result is a film that is sometimes grotesque, sometimes genuinely funny, and often deeply uncomfortable. It is also a film about an idea that horror has always understood better than polite society does: the desire to perfect humanity is itself one of humanity’s most dangerous imperfections. In its final chapter, Breeder does not offer an easy escape from that contradiction, it leaves us inside it.
-JM
Narrative Shorts: Crushing
Gender Studies
Gender Studies centers protagonist Maisie, a trans student who is enamored with her classmate Rachel. Maisie quietly observes Rachel’s mannerisms in the classroom setting, as Rachel embodies femininity and is an object of desire. The core of Gender Studies ultimately centers around desire, the desire to present a certain way, the desire to be desirable. There is a good bit of thematic depth to Gender Studies. My favorite piece of subtext in Gender Studies exists within a restroom exchange between Maisie and Rachel about Rachel’s lipgloss. The film loosely flirts with an allegory to Edith Wharton’s novel, The Age of Innocence. Performances are strong from both Fanny Massarsky as Rachel and Austin Cassel as Aaron, but Jake Junkins is the standout as Maisie, bringing an icy, sensitive, and fragile interpretation of her character. I quite enjoyed this short, and it left viewers with a lot to chew on.
Fantasies of a Rescue Dummy
This short beautifully depicts an inanimate object’s desire to be pursued, to be chased after, to be held tightly as a lifeguard scoops them up from the floor of the pool and brings them back up to the surface. Fantasies of a Rescue Dummy is interspersed with live-action sequences rooted in “reality” and more formalistic and surrealist animated sequences, depicting the dreams and wishes of the protagonist, who, in this case, is a rescue dummy. The visuals during the animated sequence are simply gorgeous. The wash of pastels and smooth textures looked so tasty and served as the perfect contrast to the live-action moments. This is an endlessly creative and very touching short that also cashes in on a very well-earned laugh towards the end.
Following
Following is a tongue-in-cheek absurdist short that follows Ted, a lonely individual who gains an extra pep in his step once TV celebrity Melissa Joan Hart starts following him on Instagram. Following holds social media under the microscope as Ted goes to great lengths to try to peacock and change himself for Hart’s validation, someone he has never met. In his work life, Ted tries to flaunt the recent development to gain social capital… with little success. The film has a bone dry sense of humor that I found to be largely successful. Briana Pozner fully commits to the absurdism with the most demented shave-and-a-haircut ending, but I loved it. This was a fun one.
Zero, Infinity
This short chronicles a gay couple living in an eerie, quietly dystopian world where they must medically verify their sexuality to qualify for a mortgage. Contrasting the cold, sterile visuals and production design, the thematic subtext of the short emanates a warm tenderness that promises the triumph of love, even amidst state attempts to suppress its non-heteronormative forms.
Henri’s Party
This was getting an inside view into the events preceding a couple’s breakup. Lucie, an actor living in France, spends an evening with her boyfriend, Arnaud. Lucie is invited to attend a party that is celebrating the cast of her production later that night, but Arnaud insists on staying, even against Lucie’s own wishes. After a change of plans, the couple find themselves hosting the party at Lucie’s apartment, much to Arnaud’s dismay. For a short, I was very impressed with the character work and humanity and detail that director Alex O. Eaton was able to imbue into these characters. Lucie and Arnaud felt lived in, as if they had existing lives before and after these events, and we the viewers were only permitted a narrow glance into their world. Tremendous credit goes to Esther Garrel and Felix Maritaud who bring all-star performances to this production. This vaguely reminded me of some of Joachim Trier’s work with the character dynamics, European setting, and age demographics. I definitely enjoyed my viewing of this.
Neuman
If I was to highlight a single standout performance out of each of the shorts shown, it would be Carine Montbrand as Aoife. Aoife is a leader in her local AA chapter and is sent into a spiral when her friend Neuman doesn’t show on his one year anniversary of sobriety. Aoife reaches a point of complete exhaustion, and Montbrand delivers a performance that shows someone who is emotionally depleted. There were some subtle shades of Mike Leigh in this film that I sensed from both the shot composition, not to mention the nucleus of the film surrounding a rough-around-the-edges lead who, by the end of the film, slowly turns a corner. While much of the runtime of Neuman feels undeniably bleak, the film possesses tremendous heart. Aoife is gruff, and a bit unpleasant. She struggles to accept love, but badly needs it, just like all of us. She is shown love, kindness, and patience when her temper is short, and people pick up slack for her when she needs it. Writer and director T.J. Elledge does a masterful job of toeing the line allowing viewers to walk alongside Aoife and her struggle while also showing the tender moments of love and connection that keep her grounded. There are also several jokes that landed. Neuman is a mature and methodical film that contains some terrific character work and writing and was met with enthusiastic applause at the credits, well deserved.
The Dream is a Snail
I only got to see about four minutes of this short before we were surprised with a buffer screen that would not load. It was pretty disappointing and a bit anticlimactic as this was the final showing of the event, but technical difficulties happen, and you roll with the punches! From what I did see, the visuals were very impressive, boasting sharp cinematography and excellent lighting. The short had a propulsive momentum that seemed to compound as time went on. I wish I could have caught the rest of it!
-EM
Tennessee Shorts: Strange Detours
This collection of shorts offers a sneak peek into the lives of a diverse assortment of characters, from guardian angels to honky-tonk musicians. It’s the perfect collection for anyone looking for something a little different.
My $3,500 Jacket
Directed and written by Drew Maynard, this hilarious 13-minute film is inspired by his own personal experiences. We meet a man who thrifts an expensive Italian designer jacket, and all he wants is for someone to compliment him on it. During the Q&A, Maynard explained that he and his friends created the film to give people a good laugh, and it certainly delivers. The main character travels to several locations around Nashville, fishing for compliments on his jacket. Sarcastic and witty, the film reminds us how easily we can make a mountain out of a molehill.
Falling Short
This short, directed by Allison Alonzo and written by EmElise Knapp, tells the story of a lazy guardian angel whose human meets an untimely death under his less-than-careful watch. Alonzo explained in the Q&A following the screening that the short film won Best Film of 2026 at the Nashville 48 Film Project, a competition known for its strict two-day timeline and specific requirements for characters, lines, and props. It’s a laugh-out-loud comedic take on angels, devils, and their fall from grace, turning an otherwise serious topic upside down.
The Sisters of Scott County
Directed by Courtney Hoffman, in this short we meet Cherry Mary Reedy, one of five redheaded, moonshine-making sisters. All Cherry Mary wants is to pass her driver’s license test so she can become a trucker, but things take a turn when the girls get into trouble with some local rivals. My favorite aspect of this film was the dynamic between the sisters. The actresses did an amazing job portraying not only the banter, squabbling, and shirt-stealing that come with having sisters, but also the love and teamwork that go along with it. This 12-minute short is the perfect mix of sisterhood, ‘70s vibes, car chases, and witty dialogue, making for an incredibly fun film.
KPOP Smoothie
Is directed by and starring Haneol Lee, and tells the story of a broke young Korean American intern (aka an errand boy) who orders two K-pop-themed smoothies. The only catch? They’re ridiculously overpriced, and now he’s got to find a way to get out of paying for them. As expected, this laugh-out-loud short takes an unexpected turn, ending on a completely different note. During the Q&A, Lee explained that this nine-minute film not only plays on familiar K-pop stereotypes but also explores the realities of Korean American identity in the West.
The Way Back Down
Directed and written by Nichole Lim, this was my personal favorite. We meet Annie, a woman in her thirties who moves back to her hometown following a divorce. She reconnects with Rob, an old friend from middle school, and together, they rediscover their childhood selves. This nine-minute film feels warm, cozy, and heartfelt. The chemistry between the actors is incredible; you can practically feel the sparks just by watching them. Their conversations about feeling stuck in life and as though they’re falling behind really struck a chord with me. It was so relatable that I found myself tearing up a little. As Lim herself described it during the Q&A following the screening, the film is a love letter to our childhood selves, reminding us that sometimes, when life gets heavy, all we need to do to lighten the load is revisit the things that made us happy as kids.
Best Kept Secret
This short, directed by Sarah Vansandt and written by Jamison Braly, completely shifts the mood. A young couple goes mattress shopping at a sketchy store, only to find themselves trapped inside after hours. A cheery yet sinister voice over the PA system uses their personal secrets to blackmail them into buying a mattress, making it their only way out. From the very beginning, there’s a sense that something more sinister is at play. The suspense in this 12-minute film is conveyed exceptionally well through its lighting, camera angles, and sound effects. And, of course, a twist at the end wraps everything up in a perfectly unsettling way.
If You Really Love Me, Outlive Me
Last but not least, this short, directed and written by Alejandro Ruax and Saskia d’Altena, tells the story of Texas honky-tonk musician Dale Watson and his wife, Celine Lee. Through interview clips and performance footage, this 20-minute film beautifully captures their love for each other and their love for making music. Watson used Celine as his muse for every song, even after her death, hence the song and title of the short If You Really Love Me, Outlive Me. Personal and heartfelt, it’s the kind of biographical documentary that stays with you long after the credits roll.
-CG
A Talent for Murder: An Enthralling Thriller
I chose A Talent for Murder because of the poster: it has the word “Murder” in the title and shows Helen Mirren. She plays the real-life author Patricia Highsmith in a fictional story. If you aren’t familiar with her books, that’s fine, because the film mentions her accomplishments quite quickly and naturally right at the start. You’ve probably seen the 1999 film The Talented Mr. Ripley, which is a darkly captivating thriller and an adaptation of her Ripley book series. If you plan on watching this film (which you should), it would be helpful to watch The Talented Mr. Ripley first, since his character is important to the plot of this film. You can totally get by without it, but you won’t have quite as much fun.
A Talent for Murder is set in 1995. Patricia Highsmith is an elderly American author living alone in Switzerland. Edward Ridgeway is a young man sent out from her New York publishing company to convince her to write a sixth book in the Ripley series. While he meets a charming young heiress named Clementine on the train, he arrives at Highsmith’s house to find her anything but charming: bitter and unkind, she explains to him that one of the reasons she left New York was racial bigotry (although that’s certainly not her wording).  It doesn’t take long for the viewer to see that it’s not just non-whites she hates, it’s everyone.
While she initially wants to kick Ridgeway out of her house, he manages to engage her in conversation about her character Ripley, discussing the draw of the series. Ripley, the utterly amoral protagonist, is a person who stops at nothing to get what he wants, and possesses the ability that all of us admire: he completely recreates himself as needed to achieve his goals. Highsmith begins to discuss the possibility of a plot with Ridgeway. We the audience get to see their plot acted out, with Ridgeway playing Ripley, and Clementine playing an even wealthier heiress; a story within a story. As these plots begin to show similarities, it’s clear that the film is playing with the way that our admiration for fictional characters alter the way we portray ourselves, for the better and for the worse.
While most lovers of mystery and thriller are familiar with Agatha Christie and Alfred Hitchcock, those creators inhabited an old-world gentry that most Americans are alien to and would love to experience (think of the fun, deliberately manufactured atmosphere of the Knives Out films). Ridgeway, out of his element in Europe, is quite relatable in his obvious longings to be cultured and foreign.
The factor in the mystery and thriller genres that always draws me back is the way they are adventurous and exciting, while prompting contemplation: we all have good and evil within us, and these moral elements present themselves in different ways, with different dangers and temptations. Were we in the protagonist’s place, would we share the same strengths or weaknesses? Would we kill, lie, steal, if the moment was right– or wrong? Stories like this allow us to see ideas and ideals play out, our “what ifs” asked and answered. A Talent for Murder deals with these “what ifs” in two layers, with its parallel plots, and this method makes this film its own thing, rather than mere IP-harvesting of a deceased author’s works. The lack of fawning admiration for Highsmith (a fascinatingly horrible person) helps this film’s independence as well.
Casting Helen Mirren as Highsmith is ideal. Not only is she a wonderful talent, but she possesses the special skill to play difficult people in a way that doesn’t downplay their flaws, while adding an interesting something to them that makes you dislike them but yet enjoy watching them. Alden Ehrenreich as Ridgeway/Ripley has the talent for disarming awkwardness (see him in a one-minute clip from the funniest scene in Hail, Caesar!) and sinister suavity that allows him to muddle our perceptions of his character. Olivia Cooke’s two versions of Clementine are distinct, charming but not likeable, with an ingenuous worldliness that is fun to watch.
Australian playwright Joanna Murray-Smith adapted her stage play Switzerland into A Talent for Murder, and the film is directed by the Dutch director Anton Corbijin, who made the 2010 film The American with George Clooney. The music is by Dickon Hinchliffe, of the alternative rock group Tindersticks, and it is simultaneously heavy-handed and exactly perfect for the film.
Robbie Ryan’s cinematography develops the theme, with color and lighting, and shots that capture imaginative uses of reflections in windows and mirrors to underscore themes. Shots are constrained in certain moments, expansive in others, and the depiction of old money in Europe have all the glamor and tantalizing allure for the audience that they have for the characters. Objects have textures, from fabrics, paperbacks, glasses and bottles, cigarette smoke and Fabergé eggs. The tactile nature of the film makes you want to be able to touch the objects as well, and this longing to sense what is depicted works smoothly with the envious sensuality of the story.
I had a wonderful time watching the film, forgetting that I sat in a movie theater and transported into the beautifully dark world of Patricia Highsmith. I recommend it to all. The film will have its US theatrical release on October 23rd.
-GK
