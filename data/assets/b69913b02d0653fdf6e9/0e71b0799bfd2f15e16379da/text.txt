Donna Isham’s Transformation and Beauty: When a Painting Begins to Move
Joseph E. Morgan |
October 11, 2026
There is something inherently appealing about the idea of transformation in art. A painting changes as an artist works on it. A musical idea changes when it is performed. A photograph freezes something that was once moving. And sometimes, when artists from different disciplines work together, the artwork itself changes into something neither could have made alone. That idea is at the heart of Donna Isham’s Transformation and Beauty, now on view at Soulios Gallery. The exhibition, which marks Isham’s Nashville debut, brings together abstract and figurative paintings, sculpture, and an immersive installation created in collaboration with her husband, composer Mark Isham. The paintings provide the foundation for the exhibition, but the immersive work gives Transformation and Beauty its most intriguing dimension by asking what happens when a painting is allowed to move, change, and exist in time.
Isham’s paintings are physical objects. She works with acrylic, oil stick, graphite, charcoal, cold wax, paper, ink, pumice, and other materials, building surfaces through accumulation, erasure, and layering. There is a sense that these paintings have been worked over rather than simply painted. Marks remain visible; some forms emerge while others recede. Color can be lush and beautiful, but there is usually something underneath it—a roughness, a line, a fragment of another image—that keeps the surface from becoming merely decorative.
Donna Isham, Paris, 2018, Oil on Canvas, 60 x 48 in
Isham’s familiarity with the history of modernism is also apparent. As Steven Soulios pointed out at the artist talkback last weekend, You Are My Calm and Paris, clearly carry the influence of Egon Schiele and Viennese Expressionism. Isham’s treatment of the human figure, particularly the elongated forms, expressive lines, and sense of psychological rather than merely physical presence, recalls Schiele without becoming imitation. Her figures seem less interested in simply presenting the body than in suggesting the emotional life within it.
The comparison is useful because Isham’s figures often seem caught between self-possession and exposure. They are not simply people being represented; they seem to be people experiencing something. Elsewhere, Isham moves decisively away from the figure. Her more abstract paintings bring to mind Wassily Kandinsky, particularly in their interest in the expressive possibilities of color, line, and shape independent of literal representation. Her shapes can seem to float, collide, and converse across the surface, while color establishes relationships that do not require a recognizable subject.
I would not suggest that Isham is simply working in a Schiele or Kandinsky tradition. The history of modern art is too complicated for such easy genealogies. But those comparisons provide useful points of entry into the works of an artist who seems to be asking some of the fundamental questions of modernity: How expressive can a line become? Can color communicate without representing something? What happens when the human figure becomes more psychological than anatomical? And how much can an artwork communicate without telling us exactly what it means?
Donna Isham, Carousel, 2023, Acrylic, ink, graphite on canvas, 61 x 71 1_2 – By Donna Isham – (Immersive Painting without Animation)
Transformation
Isham’s background in film, costume design, and styling is also worth considering. There is a cinematic quality to much of the work. Her paintings can feel like fragments of a larger story, as though we have entered a scene after something has already happened or just before something is about to occur. They do not necessarily tell us the story; they give us enough to imagine one. That quality becomes especially important in In the Darkness…I Could Hear Your Voice, the immersive installation that pushes Isham’s interest in transformation into genuinely new territory. Originally conceived as a large-scale painting, the work has been transformed through projection-mapped animation and an original score by Mark Isham. The painting becomes light, movement, and sound. What began as a static image now unfolds in time.
Isham’s explanation of how the work developed makes the transformation more meaningful. She told me that In the Darkness…I Could Hear Your Voice “began with the intention of becoming something larger than a painting.” Like much of her expressionist work, she said, it “evolved organically.” She began making marks with graphite and building layers of imagery, and as the painting developed, “it began to speak back to me, guiding what I would add, remove, or paint over.” Eventually, “a face began to emerge from the dark background,” and the title came to her almost immediately. From there, she says, she “simply followed the work and allowed it to unfold.”
The title, Isham explained, “felt so poetic and emotionally charged that it naturally invited the introduction of music and animation.” That description is important, suggesting that the possibility of movement was already contained, at least emotionally, within the painting itself. Circular imagery, a recurring motif in her paintings, gave the animation a visual language to extend, creating “a sense of movement and continuity.” Mark Isham’s music then added another emotional dimension. The work is about “longing for someone who is no longer present, and the way memory can transcend distance and time.” That is a particularly evocative subject for an artwork that moves between stillness and motion. Isham describes the experience of hearing a familiar voice as something that can suddenly make someone feel close “even when you are separated by miles or years.” In that context, the music and animation do not merely embellish the painting. They give physical form to the instability of memory itself, allowing something absent to seem present again.
Isham believes that is precisely what the immersive elements make possible. “I think the animation and music allowed the painting to express something that was already there emotionally but couldn’t be fully realized in a static image,” observing that “They gave those feelings movement, rhythm, and a sense of time, allowing the viewer to experience the work in a more intimate and immersive way.” That distinction makes In the Darkness…I Could Hear Your Voice more interesting than a painting with a technological overlay. The work is not simply being animated; the painting is being allowed to discover another dimension of itself. Forms can appear and disappear. Light can move across the surface. A mark that seemed fixed becomes part of something fluid. With Mark Isham’s music added to the experience, the work acquires duration. We are no longer simply looking at a painting; we are experiencing the painting as an event.
Donna and Mark Isham in front of In The Darkness I Can Hear Your Voice (photo: Jake Isham)
What makes this particularly interesting is that Isham does not believe every painting should undergo that transformation. Ethereal Dreamer, for example, is a work she feels is complete in its original form. In particular, she does not believe the woman at the center of the painting should be coupled with music and animation. There is something revealing about that decision. For Isham, immersion is not simply a technological effect to be applied indiscriminately. It has to serve the particular character of the work. Some paintings need to move; others need to remain still. Isham is not attempting to make every painting bigger, louder, or more spectacular because she can. Instead, she seems to be asking a more fundamental question: What does this particular painting want to become?
That question also helps explain the collaboration with Mark Isham. The relationship between visual art and music has a long history, but the collaboration between husband and wife adds another historical layer to the exhibition. 19th Century Romantics Fanny Mendelssohn and Wilhelm Hensel lived in a world in which music and visual art occupied very different professional spaces, yet Hensel’s drawings and Fanny’s compositions existed within the same intimate creative environment. Man Ray and Juliet Browner—later Juliet Man Ray—similarly inhabited a world in which photography, painting, film, fashion and design constantly crossed boundaries. And, in a very different cultural moment, John Lennon and Yoko Ono made their marriage part of an artistic collaboration that deliberately blurred the distinction between individual authorship and shared creation.
What makes these relationships interesting is not simply that the artists were married. It is that the marriage created a space in which two artistic languages could influence and build from one another. That is what happens here. Mark Isham does not simply provide background music for Donna’s paintings. His music becomes another way of interpreting them, just as the paintings give his music a visual environment.
Beauty
The title Transformation and Beauty therefore begins to take on several meanings. There is the transformation that takes place on the canvas as Isham builds and revises her surfaces. There is the transformation of a painting into an immersive experience. And there is the transformation that occurs when two artistic disciplines encounter one another. But Isham’s understanding of beauty gives the second half of the title a significance beyond visual attractiveness. When I asked her what beauty meant to her in the exhibition, she described it as “such an intangible and timeless concept” that goes “far beyond physical appearance.” For Isham, beauty is found in “courage, strength, individuality, vulnerability, warmth, and passion.” These are the qualities that inspire her, she said, and through her paintings she wants “to honor and celebrate them.”
Donna Isham, Ethereal Dreamer, 2025, Acrylic, graphite, oil stick on canvas, 42×44
That idea is particularly important in relation to Isham’s figurative work and her interest in what she calls the female “Gaze,” which is also the title of her new immersive work. For centuries, Isham points out, women were predominantly depicted through the male gaze, often as subjects to be admired or observed. “In my paintings, I want to shift that perspective,” she told me. “The women I paint have their own presence, strength, and individuality. They are not simply there to be looked at. They are looking directly back at you, engaging with the viewer on their own terms.” That reciprocal relationship changes the dynamic between subject and viewer. The woman in the painting is not merely available to our gaze; she possesses her own agency within the encounter.
It also complicates the conventional idea of beauty. Isham is certainly interested in beauty as something she consciously explores, but she does not want to dictate exactly what the viewer should find beautiful. “I also want the viewer to discover it for themselves,” she said. “I hope people spend time with the paintings, connect with the individuals portrayed, and recognize something meaningful or familiar within them.” Her final description of beauty is perhaps the most revealing: “For me, beauty is not just something we see. It’s something we feel, something that connects us to one another.” That conception fits the exhibition better than a definition based simply on attractive surfaces. Isham’s paintings can be beautiful, but their more interesting moments are those in which beauty is complicated by texture, ambiguity, vulnerability, or disruption. A figure may be recognizable and then disappear into abstraction. A luminous color may be interrupted by a rough mark. A still image may suddenly begin to move.
For me, the exhibition is at its best when these things happen and we are allowed to experience the work without being told exactly what they mean. Isham is clearly interested in large subjects—memory, identity, connection, vulnerability, longing, and transcendence—but she doesn’t seem to need to resolve those questions for us. Instead, she gives us a way to think about them through the physical experience of looking and, in the immersive works, listening too.
Isham’s show continues through December, and in one of the Nashville Art Scene’s happy coincidences, it is just down the block from the Frist Museum whose Shimmer exhibition features a number of works that might also be considered immersive, but certainly in a different way. I’d suggest that, while you have the chance, go check out both!
