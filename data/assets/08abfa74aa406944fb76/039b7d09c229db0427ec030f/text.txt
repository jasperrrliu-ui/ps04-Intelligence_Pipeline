Hello and welcome to Behind the Ballet. I’m Elizabeth Kaye, and today we’ll explore the
three captivating ballets in our Fall Season’s Program titled Neoclassical Works. One is a
pioneering contemporary piece created on ABT’s dancers by the bold and gifted Kyle Abraham whose
work has the force of a tornado and challenges our great dancers while giving them new opportunities
to shine. The other two are exquisitely gracious ballets created by master choreographers born nine
months apart in 1904: Frederick Ashton and George Balanchine. Both men became revered for their
reinterpretations of classical ballet which they reworked in accordance with their own aesthetic.
Balanchine diverged from the classical ballet he learned and danced in Russia by creating
works that endowed his dancers with a sharp angularity and an off-kilter quality coupled
with a speed that could be achieved only in the 20th century. Ashton gave his dancers a new and
sensual upper-body expressiveness that featured gracefully angled positioning, crystalline arms,
and soft backs. To this, he added fleet, intricate footwork and many swift changes of direction and
this all resulted in a uniquely British style of dance. In these ways, Ashton fathered ballet in
Britain, just as Balanchine became the father of American ballet. One aspect of Ashton’s genius
was his choreography for women and the supreme graciousness and allure he brought forth in his
ballerinas is one of many reasons his ballet in this program, Birthday Offering, is such a delight
for our eyes. He created Birthday Offering in 1956 to celebrate the 25th anniversary and remarkable
progress of Britain’s first ballet company, The Vic-Wells Ballet, which was about to receive its
Royal Charter and henceforth be known as The Royal Ballet. At the time, the company had seven stellar
ballerinas and Birthday Offering, which Ashton described as “a ballet in the grand manner,” was
designed to showcase the most dazzling qualities of each of them. In 1956, Birthday Offering’s
leading ballerina was Margot Fonteyn, who was Ashton’s muse and also The Royal Ballet’s prima
ballerina assoluta, which is the loftiest rank a female dancer can attain. Fonteyn rarely coached
other dancers, but when ABT presented Birthday Offering in 1990, which was the company’s 50th
anniversary, it was Fonteyn who coached the ballerina performing her role, Susan Jaffe,
who, as you know, is now ABT’s Artistic Director. In Birthday Offering, as our seven ballerinas and
their cavaliers come waltzing onto the stage to Glazunov’s lilting music, Ashton beguiles us with
their sheer loveliness. When the waltz concludes, the seven men stand sentry-like at the rear stage
as each ballerina performs a solo. Incidentally, at The Royal Ballet, the nickname for this
ballet was Seven Brides for Seven Brothers. The solos Ashton created for his seven ballerinas
were designed to highlight each dancers’ unique character, capabilities, and style, and in fact,
every ballerina is unique because achieving the exalted rank of ballerina, otherwise known
as Principal Dancer, takes more than performing ballet steps wonderfully; you must also imprint
those steps with your own personality, with your heart, and even with your soul. And that is what
ABT’s seven ballerinas do as, one by one, they dance alone. In one solo, a spritely ballerina
darts and lunges and spins; another ballerina displays her unusual strength and control.
One solo is fiery, while another is beautifully expansive and poised and all seven are as distinct
as the ballerinas performing them. These solos are followed by a mazurka for the men who dance with
such adroit musicality that you would never guess that this mazurka is extremely difficult to perform.
Then comes the flowing pas de deux for the leading ballerina and her cavalier which, with
its lovely balances, arching arabesques and gentle lifts, and nuanced partnering, all of
which add up into a wondrous foray into romance made visible. In the celebratory finale, as the
seven ravishing couples, with arms outstretched, so rhythmically and buoyantly traverse the stage,
Birthday Offering, created 70 years ago, shows us anew that the beauty of ballet is as timeless as
it is heart-stirring and uplifting. The second piece on the program, Kyle Abraham’s arresting
Mercurial Son, is another marvelous showcase for dancers. This unusually dynamic work,
performed to an avant-garde electronic score, is a whirlwind of dance that veers between the
precision of classical ballet and ferocious contemporary movement. When Abraham created
Mercurial Son for ABT’s spectacular dancers, he knew that their uncommon and remarkable range
of skills would enable them to perform a work in which they must move with the force of breaking
waves and the shocking speed of a fast-spreading fire and do this to music that features tricky
rhythmic challenges. Watching the dancers spin and swirl with an eye-popping combination of abandon
and control, shows how aptly titled Mercurial Son is, for these extraordinary dancers embody what it
means to be mercurial. From the moment the curtain rises on the third work, George Balanchine’s
Ballet Imperial, you’ll be enveloped in beauty and magic. Because in this, as in every ballet,
two crucial elements of that beauty and magic are the sets and costumes which create a world and a
mood before a single step is performed or a single note is sounded. More than in most ballets, Ballet
Imperial has undergone significant costume and set changes since it premiered in 1941. Initially,
the set recalled the Tsar’s Winter Palace in St. Petersburg, with the River Neva in the distance,
and the elegant costumes called forth the Imperial era of Balanchine’s youth, before the
revolution changed everything. But in the early 1960s, Balanchine returned to Russia for the
first time in nearly 40 years and concluded that nobody understands what Imperial means anymore.
So, he stripped away those visual references, simplifying the set and costuming the women in
unadorned chiffon dance skirts. But when you see ABT’s dancers perform this masterwork, you’ll see
a new set and gorgeous new costumes created for our company by Jean-Marc Puissant, a former dancer
who has become an internationally hailed designer. The bold, glowing colors he chose range from
brilliant gold to enticing, ever-deepening shades of blue that resonate under the blazing lights of
the stage and restore that sense of majesty the ballet originally exuded. Yet in each of Ballet
Imperial’s visual iterations, the Tchaikovsky music and the choreography remained the same, and
what makes this possible is that Ballet Imperial has no plot and therefore was one of the first
ballets to establish a mood and engender emotion simply through the dancing. Another unchanged
element is the tone of this ballet which is rich with the graciousness that permeated the grand
19th century Russian ballets of Petipa and Tchaikovsky, The Sleeping Beauty, The Nutcracker, and Swan Lake,
to which Ballet Imperial pays homage. Ballet Imperial begins with the dancers bowing to
one another, and that courtly gesture immediately draws us into the otherworldly ambiance Balanchine
devised and then heightens as dancers move in lush patterns augmented with stunning virtuosic
passages. One achingly lovely moment occurs when a male dancer offers his hand to one woman,
then to another, as all ten women on the stage join hands and he leads them forward to the
impossibly gentle music. As Balanchine proved time and again, no plot is necessary when the dancing
alone leaves you marveling at the beauty you’ve witnessed. What the three works in this program
show us is that ballet steps are the equivalent of an alphabet through which choreographers produce
their own form of language, and beyond that, their own form of enchantment. The ingenious ways those
carefully chosen steps are woven together gives us more than a dance: they give us a world. A world
that is entirely separate from the everyday world we inhabit, and in that sense what ballet gives
us is a uniquely bewitching art… and a sanctuary. Thank you for joining me for Behind the Ballet.
