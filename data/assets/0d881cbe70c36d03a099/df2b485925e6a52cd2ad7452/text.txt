Tosca: The Innocence of Art and the Violence of Power
Joseph E. Morgan |
October 15, 2026
On October 8 and 10, Nashville Opera gave an outstanding performance of Giacomo Puccini’s Tosca at the Tennessee Performing Arts Center. The opera has a long history in Music City. The earliest performance I could find was an “Operalogue”—a simplified or condensed version of the opera—presented by the Centennial Club Music Department in May 1919. In November of 1963, the Community Concert Association performed Tosca at the War Memorial Auditorium “…in understandable English.” In 1983, Michael Rennidon directed the work with the Nashville Opera Guild, a forerunner of Nashville Opera, under the baton of Nashville Symphony Orchestra maestro Michael Charry. This production, in 2026, was, according to Director John Hoomes, “…at least his fourth Nashville production,” and it was marvelous: relevant, beautiful, and everything opera should be.
Cavaradossi and Tosca embrace (Photo: Chad Driver)
Set in Rome in 1800 at the height of the Napoleonic Wars, the opera opens inside the Basilica of Sant’Andrea della Valle where our hero, the artist Mario Cavaradossi (tenor Ben Gulley), is working on a painting of Mary Magdalene when an escaped partisan, Cesare Angelotti (bass-baritone David Crawford), suddenly enters the scene. Here Cavaradossi learns that Angelotti’s sister, Marchesa Attavanti, has been coming to the basilica and recognizes the woman as someone he has seen praying intently in the basilica. Soon thereafter, Floria Tosca (soprano Brittany Olivia Logan) arrives and becomes jealous of the possibility that another woman might have been there.
So far, all is quite innocent, even the painting. Cavaradossi’s Mary has blue eyes and long blond hair, as tradition would have her. When Tosca questions him as to why Mary should have dark eyes like hers, it is out of a simple, even sweet, petulant jealousy. It is from this innocent moment that the dramatic crescendo of the violence and tragedy of Puccini’s opera begins its ascent.
Baron Scarpia (photo: Chad Driver)
During the next scene the sinister Baron Scarpia (baritone SeungHyeon Baek) arrives at the church looking for Angelotti. His entrance, first unaccompanied, then with the rumbling low double bassoons marks the end of any innocence. The presence of a folding fan with the Attavanti family crest on it increases his suspicion, particularly of Cavaradossi, suspicions that are cinched when he sees the painting and recognizes the likeness of Attavanti in the portrait. Indeed, Cavaradossi had admitted the likeness is a result of his subconscious painting the woman he saw praying during the week. Baek’s Scarpia sings with a dark and fierce relish for Cavaradossi’s blood and Tosca’s favor as he insists the children’s choir sing their Te Deum. Accompanied by a sneering and fantastically vile Spoletta (tenor Rick Hale) the scene, and act, climaxes at the height of the Te Deum, when Scarpia exclaims “Tosca, mi fai dimenticare Iddio!” (“Tosca, you make me forget God!”)
The Act’s finale is a throwback to the spectacle of old Grand Opera and in Nashville it featured the beautiful hand-painted backdrops and luxurious costumes including the Swiss Guards and the Cardinal’s procession. The setting is simple, beautiful and without technological distraction. In his preparations for the score, Puccini had consulted with Father Pietro Panichelli regarding the order of the Cardinal’s procession for the Te Deum, the correct chants that would have been sung, and he had traveled himself to Rome to get the correct pitches and dynamics of the bells for each scene. All of this was performed with a patient, nuanced attention to detail by Maestro Dean Williamson. With acts that create a dramatic crescendo, conductors can tend to rush, but here Williamson allowed us to take delight in the beautiful scenes and costumes, even as we languished under Scarpia’s evil remonstrations.
Importantly, Cavaradossi’s art is naïve and apolitical, but Puccini’s is not.
With the commencement of Act Two, now in Scarpia’s chambers at the Palazzo Farnese, the opera’s central terror begins as Scarpia tortures Cavaradossi in front of Tosca, while both are questioned. It’s a horrid scene, watching Tosca’s despair increase as the torture of Cavaradossi intensifies. Puccini, writing at the end of the 19th century, was expressing a political sentiment, that violence that can come from a police state, even or especially at its collapse. It is a powerful, chilling scene, even for an American audience that has seen the photographs from Abu Ghraib and many events since.
Tosca’s Kiss!
The graphic nature of the violence and murder at the end of the second act connect the opera, albeit tenuously, to the verismo movement in opera that was happening simultaneously in Italy at the end of the 19th Century. The movement was a post-Romantic tradition that abandoned the conventions of bel canto, and that featured raw human emotions of common folk (soldiers, artists, factory workers) as they confront the all-encompassing powers of tradition, religion and culture. Typically, they do not end well for anyone. Puccini shares verismo’s taste for theatrical immediacy, extreme emotional situations, and violence, but Tosca also retains the concentrated theatrical construction and musical sophistication of late-Romantic opera.
Puccini’s determination to maintain dramatic momentum worked hand in hand with his avoidance of bel canto convention. He avoided character development of all but his central character as well as the conventional numbers expected by his contemporaries. Julian Budden has pointed out that Puccini cut a quartet for Act Two on the “…grounds that it reverted to the static pezzo concertato convention” of the past. Puccini’s printer, Ricordi, demanded a duet in Act Three between Cavaradossi and Tosca, a demand that went unmet. Even Joseph Kerman’s famous later description of the work as a “shabby little shocker” is largely attributed to its dependence on graphic violence and sensationalism with a distinct lack of character development.
They’re not wrong: Cavaradossi and Scarpia receive little if any development, and their caricature is exaggerated in either direction (for good or evil). Only Tosca’s character changes, but when it happens, it’s epic. In her famous “Vissi d’arte” at the culmination of the second act, sung magnificently by Logan whose warm, flexible instrument riveted the room, Tosca’s innocence dissipates into the thin air. Logan’s high B-flat, appended by one of opera’s most famous singhiozzando (a sobbing directed in the score) was absolutely heart-rending. Tellingly, after killing Scarpia, when Tosca exclaimed “Or gli perdono” (“now I forgive him”) women throughout the theater chuckled.
Cavaradossi laments (photo: Chad Driver)
The 3rd act opens with a wonderful pastoral sung by a Shepherd Boy that resets the energy back to a peaceful idyll. This was sung excellently by Amayah Rodriguez on Saturday, but my informants tell me that Gwen Whitaker was also excellent as the Shepherd Boy on Thursday. This was followed by Cavaradossi’s giant, lamenting ‘E lucevan le stelle.’ Todd Waldecker’s clarinet gets special mention here for a beautifully played opening. Gulley’s line was beautiful. He managed the heights of the line with a silvery nostalgia that slowly evolved into a powerful, brassy voice that exquisitely cut through Puccini’s orchestration, itself balanced perfectly by Maestro Williamson. The generous resonance reminded me of my mother’s old recordings of José Carreras in the same role.
Then came the execution. For non-dramatic reasons which need no mention, Director Hoomes altered the ending rather ingeniously (at the talk back he called it a “Pulp-Fiction” ending), but Cavaradossi was nevertheless double-crossed and executed publicly by firing squad–the verismo fine tragica obtained. As Tosca climbed atop the battlements, I could feel the emotion welling in my heart and a quick glance around me found an audience full of sparkling eyes.
Julian Budden, an otherwise staunch champion of Puccini, has attacked Puccini’s “inept handling of the political element,” calling for more context from the Napoleonic Wars. Yet, as any painter will tell you, the less figure, the more abstract, the more interpretive possibilities and the greater universal and personal impact of the work. This might be one of the greatest powers of Tosca—no matter the time or place, and the moral lesson of violence in a police state will ring true in largely different circumstances. Since World War Two the opera has often, and quite convincingly, been set in Nazi occupied Rome (this despite puzzling evidence that Puccini embraced Mussolini).
During the talkback, one wise audience member asked the cast what the audience should take away from the historical background of the opera. Pointedly, Gulley remarked that no matter what time or place, there is always a Scarpia, and Director Hoomes, ever careful around the political nature of the work, remarked that it is the drama that makes the work a masterpiece. There is no way that Director Hoomes could have known that the U.S. Secretary of Defense and the Pentagon would announce plans to publicly stream an execution by firing squad the week he produced Tosca (since cancelled by the President), but the parallels are hard to ignore. Indeed, Hoomes’s intention in producing Tosca this season might be as naïve and apolitical as the blue eyes in Cavaradossi’s painting, but art, like history, has a way of revealing what we would rather not see. Nashville Opera returns with Opera on the Mountain, November 6th.
