A Shimmering Display At Frist
Bethany Morgan |
October 11, 2026
Shimmer: Dreaming the Posthuman is Frist Art Chief Curator’s Mark Scala’s last exhibit before retiring, and what a fascinating exhibit it is. Shimmer: Dreaming the Posthuman features 17 artists working with both analog and emergent technologies to visualize the interrelationship between humanity, other species, and the earth itself. Posthumanism challenges the humanist idea that humans are the center of the universe and above other lifeforms. The artists in this exhibition blur the lines between different forms of existence: animal, vegetable and mineral while emphasizing that human’s abilities and intellect should be used to care for our world instead of harming or destroying it in our quest for progress. Regarding the title “Shimmer,” Scala writes, “The works on view convey the quality of shimmer – a transient glow that makes things appear immaterial and otherworldly. In the gallery’s luminous space, extinct species virtually reappear; machines become entwined with amoeba, slime, and miocrobes; mysterious light-filled avatars float and dance through fantastic landscapes; and the earth reveals itself to be a living organism.” This exhibition is divided into the following sections: Endangered Species, Dreams and Metamorphosis, and Everything is Connected.
Anna Dumitriu and Alex May. ArchaeaBot: A Post Climate Change, Post Singularity Life-Form, 2018–26. Robot with 3D-printed PLA and silicone, electrical components, and microcontroller. Courtesy of the artists. © Anna Dumitriu and Alex May. Photo: Vanessa Graf/Ars Electronica
In the first section, “Endangered Species,” The Substitute by Alexandra Daisy Ginsberg is a video featuring a recreation of the northern white rhinoceros. This subspecies is on the verge of extinction because of poaching and habitat loss. The digital recreation is meant to illustrate how it’s a pale imitation of the real thing. Even if we can one day, with scientific advancements, clone the white rhinoceros, we’ve still lost something precious because of our own carelessness and greed. The Substitute reminds us that it’s more important to preserve what we have than to become advanced enough to recreate things we could have saved if we had been more conscientious of the world around us. Also in this section is ArchaeaBot by Anna Dumitriu and Alex May. This is a robot the artists created after doing research on archaea (single-celled microorganisms that are believed to represent the oldest form of life on earth). It imagines an “ultimate” species for the end of the world: something part artificial intelligence and part organic life.
Marina Zurkow. Mesocosm (Wink, TX), 2012. Custom real-time generative software animation with sound; 146-hour cycle (24-minute day, 146- hour year). Collection of the Carl & Marilynn Thoma Foundation, courtesy of bitforms gallery, New York. Image courtesy of bitforms gallery NY and the artist
In the second section, “Dreams and Metamorphosis,” there is a similarity to Surrealism with the idea that dreams can reveal truth. slimeQrawl is a two-channel video by Libby Heaney in which slime oozes through objects and people. The placard beside it states that Heaney has a PhD in quantum physics and says slime represents “potentiality, transformation and the blurred boundaries between the animate and inanimate, between life and death.” Also in this section is Marina Zurcow’s Mesocosm, a custom real-time generative software animation with sound, which shows a desert sinkhole based on a real sinkhole in the oil fields of West Texas. People in hazmat suits appear to be searching for something, while monarch butterflies flutter around. There are two empty picnic tables, and a grill nearby, along with a billboard that might be displaying some sort of storm. It’s as if we’re in an apocalyptic future where it’s no longer safe to be outside for humans, but fine for the monarch butterflies, a currently endangered species that appears to be flourishing in this future.
Amelia Winger-Bearskin. I Would Like to Be Midnight / I Would Like to Be Sky, 2023. Video; 10 min., 27 sec. Courtesy of the artist. © 2023 Amelia Winger-Bearskin. Image courtesy of the artist.
In the third section, “Everything is Connected,” explores how everything in the cosmos is connected, even the past, present, and future. In the video I WOULD LIKE TO BE MIDNIGHT / I WOULD LIKE TO BE SKY,  Amelia Winger-Bearskin uses AI to transform and alter photographs she took while driving across the United States. She uses AI to remove all signs of human life like power lines. Then, using image interpolation (distorting an image from one pixel grid to another), she makes the land appear to be taking breaths. The sounds in the video are Winger-Bearskin’s vocalizations. Also in this section is the immersive work, Floating World of the Cloud Quilt by Saya Woolfalk which takes up an entire room. Using digital and analog media, as well as sculptures, Woolfalk imagines a world made more compassionate and empathetic through what she calls, “interspecies hybridization.” In this world are figures called Empaths which are humans whose DNA has been fused with plant DNA. Perhaps the only way that humans as a whole will be kinder to the earth is if our DNA is quite literally entwined with it. Stepping into the room is psychedelic and dreamlike with it’s spinning colored images and lights.
Robin Vuchnich: In Search of Thoreau’s Flowers – The Memory of a Season is the exhibit in the Frist’s Gordon Contemporary Artists Project Gallery. This really fits in quite well with Shimmer because this overarching theme of the exhibit is how humans have impacted the land around Waldon Pond. Thereau’s collection of dried and pressed plants from the region around Waldon Pond is housed at the Harvard University Herbaria. Vuchnich drew from the Harvard collection for her video projection, This and No Mistake to create a large scale visualization of environmental change. Scala writes, “If Thoreau walked those woods today, he would encounter a profoundly altered landscape. Of Thoreau’s 648 plant specimens preserved at Harvard, over half are now extinct or endangered in the Concord region because of climate change, pollution, overdevelopment, and other human causes.” Vuchnich’s second video projection, The Memory of a Season, is an immersive projection of floral species in decline around Walden Pond. A very beautiful demonstration of the impact humanity has on our environment; there are sensors that pick up the movement of the viewer and alters the image as the person moves. When the person is still, the fields return to normal.
These exhibitions will be on display at the Frist Art Museum until January 3rd, 2027. When you go, expect to see strange and wonderful things, more thought-provoking than simply visually appealing. Also, note the use of AI in some of the works like Collective Effervescence by Marguerite Humeau and Stone Speaks by Nancy Baker Cahill. These artworks explore the idea of AI as a collaborator, but always viewing it with a critical lens. As AI begins to shape our future, we must ask ourselves what part it will play in our lives because, as we know, everything is connected.
