The MCR Interview:
Andrew Cohen: A Behind the Scenes on Set Design
Grace Krenz |
October 7, 2026
Andrew Cohen is a scenic designer who works with many theaters, including Middle Tennessee’s own Studio Tenn. He is currently working with them on their upcoming production of The Addams Family. He kindly took the time to speak with us about his role in theatrical productions.
Grace Krenz (GK): I’m excited to get to talk with you because I’m very curious about what you do as a scenic designer. Can you explain what your role entails?
Andrew Cohen (AC): I’m contracted to basically envision the world, both aesthetically and functionally. Set design is about form following function. So my primary goal is to make sure the play and the musical work, both location and setting wise: how things come on and off, where we are, the storytelling of the place, and then also how it looks and fits the theater and works with the actors and things like that. So a lot of my work is a balance between those two things. And I always hope that a successful scenic design does both of those things very well.
GK: That’s great. So walk me through it. You’re contracted for a play and they give you the book of the play. What do you do next?
AC: My typical process is usually the same across the board. I’ll get the script and I’ll read it. Usually for fun, but I also write down some stuff that I will want to remember, any important stage directions, or anything a character says about where they are or what they’re about to do or where they’re going that may not be in the stage directions. Sometimes that is left out of the stage directions and something happens with what a character says. So I’ll read the script and I’ll jot down some notes and then I’ll talk with the director. We’ll have a conversation about how we want to approach it, what do we think it looks like, and how does it work? I might get into a little more specifics at the time, like “ I was thinking the front door is on the right side of the stage? Do you agree with that?” You know, some of these basic questions like,”How do we want this play to flow?” Then maybe, depending on the play and how– I don’t want to say straightforward– but straightforward, the play or the musical is, I may have some research I’ve already pulled that I found, of what I think the set or location may look like. A lot of inspiration, period research and stuff like that, just to have some things to show the director and for us to say “Oh, it looks more like this thing and not like that thing,” which is really helpful because then I could gather more of this idea to really supplement what I’m going to do with it.
After that, if I’ve shown my research and it goes off I’ll move on to my next step. If it hasn’t, I’ll do another round of research and see if it is more of what they’re thinking?
Andrew Cohen’s Set Design for Studio Tenn’s Jersey Boys
So after all that, all the research, I start doing renderings. They used to be pencil drawings and color pencil and now things have shifted more digitally. So my renderings are a combination of 3D drafting, to get overall scale and stuff like that, where I think walls may or may not be, and then I take it into Photoshop. Photoshop’s where it comes alive for me, where I can try a bunch of colors and texture sand start placing furniture in and really start bringing the character of the set to life, which I think is always in the details and the color and the textures and all that. So I’ll do this rendering and I’ll send it to the director and we’ll have a conversation about that.
If it’s greenlit from there, I’ll start doing the other side of my job, which is a lot more nuts and bolts. I’ll start generating drafting for people to build it. I don’t build it and I don’t paint it and I don’t prop it. But I supply a lot of information so those people know how to do their job in the best way that they can. So I’ll do drafting, basically the overall size and shape of all the units, and it’ll have what the textures are and where the doors go and how tall the window is, and all of that, just how I want it to look. And then someone takes the technical part of it.
I also do something called paint elevations. I send it to the painter and it’s basically every single wall or unit that’s in the show has some sort of treatment and color on it, and I want to make sure that that is the correct color and the correct look, and I’ll sometimes supply research with it. So I’ll send that to the painter, and then I’ll also start talking with the props person to start sorting through what we can get for props and what I think it should look like and if the style feels good and the period is right. So all that goes into the shop and they start working on it.
In between, we have a lot of design team meetings to make sure things are going well, and that there’s nothing that we mixed up or missed or anything. Then they build the set.
I show up for tech. Tech is where actors are on stage, and lighting is for the first time doing their thing, and we see costumes on actors, and we’re going through the show page by page, scene by scene, making sure that it all works together in one idea. And then we go through previews where we have an audience and we run the show and see how it interacts with the audience and how the show actually runs on its feet, and then we open. So that’s typically my process.
GK: That’s really cool! How long does this usually take, in terms of hours spent on the project, and from when they first contract you till the first day of shows?
AC: It’s easier to tell you the answer to when I’m contracted. Usually it depends on the theater. I mean, ideally, I think six to nine months is a pretty good landing spot for some of these. Sometimes it’s a little quicker. Sometimes there’s a little more time. But, that’s a good range in that it gives us enough time to not rush the important parts, which I think is talking about the play and going through research,and the renderings, and making sure it looks right. The reason it’s also that far out is that it gives the shops and the painters and the people ample time to do their job, so they’re not rushed trying to build and paint because no matter what scale your set is, it’s a big undertaking. I’m contracted about 6 to 9 months out, somewhere in that range, but I’ve had sooner and I’ve had them later.
Hours on a show is hard to say. I don’t know if I’ve ever really done the math, and to be real, I don’t want to do the math because I get paid in thirds installments, so it’s not like I’m paid hourly. It takes a while. Sometimes a rendering will take me a week of pretty much straight working on it. That being said, I have other projects I’m juggling in the background. So my attention is usually focused on something, but there’s always a secondary and a tertiary project that is not related that I’m focusing on. That’s the freelance life. So the rendering takes maybe a week to do it right. And the drafting takes maybe a week of straight working on it. When I come out to tech, we’re there from noon to 10PM for a handful of days, four or five days, and the paint elevations take me maybe a day or so. I mean it’s hard to really clock the hours because, again, I’m plate-spinning a lot of projects. It takes time.
GK: How do you deal with different theater spaces? Do you generally have the same amount of space, or does that vary a lot?
AC: All the theaters are so unique. And it depends on what type of theater it is. When I say that, I mean, is it a grid theater that has a fixed grid above, or is it a fly house that has a fly rail where you could bring scenery in and out from above? Is it in the round? Is it alley? Most designers would say they design for the space. You’re designing for the play, of course, but you also design for the space. What I mean by that is the size of the theater. An Importance of Being Earnest on an 80 foot wide theater is going to look very different from an Importance of Being Earnest that’s in a 20 foot wide thrust theater. You also want to fit the space, the set wants to fit nicely in the space as well. And with all that being said, the goal for us is always to focus on the actors. So a lot of the time in these bigger theaters, our goal is to focus in, to really keep our eye where the action is, which is the actors in the play.
Andrew Cohen’s Set Design for Studio Tenn’s Jesus Christ Superstar
GK: Is there a specific setting or period or style that you find the most fun to design?
AC: The cool thing about theater is there’s a lot of variety in both the shows and how to present them. A lot of designers, and I think I would probably include myself in that, get to be a chameleon in that, one minute we’re doing Victorian, the next minute we’re doing a modern kitchen, and then the next thing we’re doing a stylized musical, like The Addams Family. But we’re always trying to be true and honest to what the core of the story is. That being said, I’ve always really loved doing shows that are set during Victorian times only because it was a great time for lots of patterns and molding and all this great detail that you don’t really see in a lot of modern shows now. A musical is really fun because you get to stylize it a little bit, like how you design for opera, you design with the spirit of the music. I have a somewhat large musical background as well. A musical is a great infusion of two of my great artistic passions. I can really take how I feel about the music and transmute it into a visual, which is really fun. So I would say probably those are my favorite. If you’ve seen some of my stuff at Studio Tenn, one of my other real favorite things is just a really well-done realistic interior that suspends your disbelief and adds a backstory to the space that hopefully, in some way gets transmitted subconsciously to the audience as well, the story behind what the space used to be, this home or this room or whatever.
GK: How do you deal with differences between designing a scene for an established play that’s been around for a while, and a new work that’s a blank slate?
AC: That’s a great question. Maybe The Addams Family is a good example of this. It’s a musical that’s being done a lot, but it’s also associated with a lot of past references. In this particular case, I leaned into it a little bit more where I was drawing on the old illustrations and I was looking at the old TV show and the movies and the Wednesday show on Netflix, but I also was influenced a bit by Tim Burton’s aesthetic as well. It’s kind of an homage to a lot of those things, but it still feels uniquely its own. And I think that’s where good research really comes in, is that you can be inspired by things of the past and tip your hat to it without actually having to copy it. And I think one of the best things about theater in its medium is that you can see Hamlet twenty different times and probably every time it’ll be very different. And I think that’s what’s great. I think that’s why people come back to the theater, there’s just variety whenever and wherever we’re doing it. There’s always something that inspires a unique version of it. And I think audiences are there for that, they understand that they’re not just seeing a cookie cutter version of the play they saw before; that’s what a Broadway tour is for. You want to see the thing that was on Broadway but you couldn’t get to it and then you can just see it wherever you are and that’s what it is. Outside of that, it’s really about variety and putting your own spin on it.
New works are interesting because they have never been done before. When you do a big musical that’s been done a lot, like The Addams Family, you know it works because people have been doing it before. And I mean that logistically. Part of my job is figuring out how does this show work? Especially if there’s a lot of different locations and it’s never been done. It’s been worked out a little bit in workshops but it’s like, “Okay, well, how do we get this whole new set of furniture on stage really quickly, because we’re in a totally different location?” And so part of that conversation with new work is, what’s our language of the play or the musical? How are we presenting this? Is there flexibility? Is it a little more abstract so that the set can move in and out or is it really realistic? At that point, no one’s ever put a visual mark on it.
I think that’s where good research comes in again, where it really can help you define a language in the style of a play or a musical. You cross your fingers a little bit, and hope that the way, the style you’ve come up with and how you’re going to present it works with the telling of the story. It’s a lot more work. I would say new works are a lot more work. Just because there is so much unknown and so much figuring out, and things change too. Things won’t change on The Addams Family because it’s been licensed and that’s how it’s presented. But in a new work a whole scene can be cut, and then they add a new scene. The whole scene could have been in the park, but the new scene is in a car and we have to figure out how to do that.
GK: That’s great. You’ve mentioned your design for Studio Tenn, The Addams Family. Can you tell us a little bit about your design for their upcoming production?
AC: Certainly. We have a lot of different scenes, but it all revolves around the Addams family house. There are scenes in the graveyard, in Central Park, and inside different rooms in their house. There’s a lot of locations and we move really quickly. It’s a typical musical in that sense where there’s a lot of variety in where we are. My inspiration, I talked a little bit about this earlier, is that a lot of my research was the original illustrations, probably that was the most inspiring, the original illustrations. Some of the TV show and some of the movies and Wednesday, and again, Tim Burton, just because of his style. Because his stuff’s really great for this type of, I don’t want to say horror. It’s not really horror, but goofy macabre fun. He’s done great things in that medium, and so that’s where a lot of my research comes from.
This was very much a how do we establish we’re in the graveyard with your Spanish oak tree versus how we show that we’re inside the house now, at their dining table. How do we get from this scene to that scene, a lot of that kind of logistical thing. The design is traditional wing and drop, which means there’s portals and it staggers back in space. They still use it on Broadway today and it’s a great way, especially in this theater, which is a great theater, to tie this all together. It doesn’t have a fly system, so it’s not easy to get big scenery. You can’t get big scenery from above because there is no above, but there’s a fixed grid. So we have a lot of tracking things that come on and off. Affixed to our grid, to get things that get on stage quickly and safely and change up the scene a lot. So there’s a lot of things in the set that are tracks that help bring the space alive and surprise us a bit.
GK: That’s really cool. This isn’t the first show you’ve done with Studio Tenn. What would be one of your favorite sets you’ve designed for them?
Andrew Cohen’s Set Design for Studio Tenn’s Deathtrap
AC: My favorite might be, I mean, I really love Deathtrap because it really transported the audience. It felt so real and it was really nicely put together and nicely executed. It worked really well for the script, which is very specific about where everything is on the set. That one I just  love how that looks and I love how it works. I think maybe one of my favorites is Cabaret, because we had the band on the sides of the deck and the audience was at some cabaret tables. Cabaret is a very good show too. It’s one of my favorites. The set, again, has a little subtle storytelling to it as well. So, yeah, I think those are my two favorites. We have just done Jesus Christ Superstar as well, and what I really loved about that is we really made our own world: costumes, set, lights, choreography directing, it felt like its own unique thing. With a unique visual language that felt still true to what the musical is, while still bringing a fresh and a little bit of a contemporary take on it while still feeling the biblical times.
GK: I’m looking at the Deathtrap photo right now; it’s fantastic! It’s really satisfying to look at, with a lot of textures, and you have a lot of color without it being cheerful. How do you get into scenic design? When did you first get interested in it, and what is the career track for someone who’s wanting to do that?
AC: Yeah, you need a good mom. You’ll see why that is. I went to undergrad at the University of South Florida, and even before that, in high school, I really wanted to do architecture. So in high school, I was taking some drafting classes and stuff like that. And so when I finally got to college and I was done doing all the gen eds, I started the architecture program. I had my first two classes and I just remember it being not what I thought it was. It was a don’t meet your heroes kind of thing. For a long time, I couldn’t figure out why that was, and I’ll tell you why I think that was. But so I went. I had my first two classes and it was not quite what I thought it was going to be. And then my mom’s like, “Well, I also noticed you need to do the master’s program as well to get certified, and that’s an additional amount of schooling on top of it.” For something that I was starting to feel not amazing about, having three or four more years on top of it seems like maybe not a great idea. So I didn’t know quite what to do. My mom was like, “Hey, I was looking around at the scheduled classes, and I see in the theater department there’s some sort of design class and they’re using CAD in there. Maybe you should just check that out.” And I was like, “Okay, I don’t know anything about that, but sure.” And so I checked it out and it was awesome. I was also taking at the time this English course, which was a required literary analysis course, which actually really helped my theater career as well.
What I started to find out that I was lacking in architecture was a sense of purpose: why I’m designing, a sense of me in the design, a sense about what I interpret from what I’ve read. And also there are limitations and boundaries in architecture, but in theater, it’s a lot more defined: the proscenium’s only this tall and this wide, and you can only go this deep and you need these things. It just worked out really great. It was what I was missing. I was missing the humanity in architecture. Theater has so much storytelling and themes and things that resonate with people and also just entertainment. We can make you laugh, we can make you cry. We could do all these things that I didn’t feel like I could get from a steel building with glass.
So that’s how I fell into it. I was tracked to get a BA in theater design, and then they were offering a BFA, which included getting to design a show there. So I did the BFA. I designed one of my first shows. My grades were getting better because I was really into what I was doing.
After that, towards the end of my undergrad, my professor at the time was really into people going to this thing called URTA. URTA basically is speed dating for young set designers and grad schools. So basically, I brought my portfolio down to New York City and set up all my stuff and people came by and they would look at my stuff when I was there. They’d sign up for times to talk with me and basically invite me to come have an interview with them in New York City. And then if they enjoyed our conversation and my work, they would invite me to their school. All these programs have some sort of tuition waived or tuition reimbursement or graduate assistantships that also pay on top of it. So it was an amazing way to get further education on the subject while not having to break the bank.
I thought it was also a great time for me because, while a lot of people at this point could go out in the world and start working for a bit and then they go to grad school, but with set design, there’s so many skills you need to harness to do your job. I felt that I had some skills, but I wished I had more to execute it. And so that’s how I landed up at University of Maryland. That’s where I got my graduate degree. What I also found there is my voice too, how to talk about art and how to be confident, and it was more than just skills. It was great at the time because it’s also a bit of a safe space to do– I don’t want to say dangerous things, but to be bold and to take risks, and to try things and to fail, and to learn from them, and it’s not like the professional world where it should hopefully be right. You’re not failing in the professional world. I got more confident there and I found out a lot about myself.
What was also great about University of Maryland was that it’s so close to DC that I was able to go to all the Smithsonian museums and get inspired by classics and modern art and sculpture, and DC has a huge theater scene. My mentor worked at a lot of these places, so I was able to go with him and be a part of these processes and see how it works and know the people. And so that was my route. I know a lot of people find theater in high school. I did band the whole time, so I didn’t have time to do more than that. But a lot of people find theater in high school and just ride that wave to where we are. But that’s how I got in here.
Andrew Cohen’s Set Design for Studio Tenn’s Cabaret
GK: That’s really cool. What do you wish that more people would notice or appreciate about good scenic design or scenic design in general?
AC: It’s a very true statement with many set designers that we’re only as good as the people we work with. I can create something beautiful, but I need the people to help bring it to life. When people sit down, they’re really enthralled with the story and the actors. Maybe some people sit back and they can recognize, “Oh, there’s a really intricate set and there’s a really intricate costumes and the lighting, it’s really great and it sounds amazing,” but there’s also people behind that too, who are running the show and backstage making the scene changes happen. And then outside of that are the people that I really love, which is the people who help bring the dream to life, the people the scene shops and the painters and the prop people and all everyone in between. There are so many people behind the curtain that are making this thing into what you see. It’s  grandeur and amazingness and a lot of hard work and a lot of time. It’s both good and bad that it gets maybe unnoticed a little bit. It’s good that we’ve done our job so well that people are taken to a new world and don’t think about anything but the show. It’s amazing, but it’s good to appreciate all these people who have put in all this time and blood, sweat, and tears to make this thing happen. I’m always really appreciative of those people and I hope that our audience members, maybe when they’re a couple days after the show, when they’ve stopped thinking about the characters and their favorite songs, can say “Oh, that was really great on a technical level, a really great show, and I wonder how many people did this.” I think that’s the thing, there’s a lot of artisans that need their cheering as well.
GK: That’s a lovely answer. Thank you so much for talking with me!
AC: This was great!
If you’re curious about Andrew Cohen’s set design, you can check out his instagram @cohendesigns, or see it in person at Studio Tenn’s upcoming production of The Addams Family October 8-25th. See their website for tickets and more information.
