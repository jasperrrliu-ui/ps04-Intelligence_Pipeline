The Nashville Rep’s John Proctor is the Villain
Grace Krenz |
October 7, 2026
It’s October, and in a subtle nod to the spooky season, The Nashville Repertory Theatre has begun their 26/27 season with Kimberly Belflower’s John Proctor is the Villain. This contemporary play is about highschoolers reading Arthur Miller’s The Crucible, which tells of the most famous witchhunts of all time. This is a fun choice, and it’s refreshing to have a non-horror adjacent play during the month of Halloween.
John Proctor is the Villain was written in 2022 and made it to Broadway in 2025 starring Sadie Sink. After receiving seven Tony nominations, it is now, with producers including Sadie Sink and Tina Fey, in development to become a film under the Broadway show’s original director, Danya Taymor.  The Nashville Repertory Theatre is performing this play in Johnson Theater, the black-box theater downstairs at TPAC, and the crowd was full when I attended opening night. The play is around one hour and forty-five minutes long, with no intermission, which is a format I really enjoy.
The play is set in a rural Georgia high school, where they deal with life, love, tragedy, and homework. It begins during Carter Smith’s AP English class as he teaches the required sex ed information (due to cuts in funding) as well as introducing The Crucible as the next work they will study. After his lecture the girls in the class discuss various subjects: Shelby, Raelynn’s former best friend and classmate who left town unexpectedly after sleeping with Raelynn’s boyfriend; how hot Carter Smith is; and the formation of a feminist club at their school. As the semester continues, we see their lives alter as personal events and public scandals rock their school, altering their analysis of The Crucible. Going into the show, I was afraid that most of the play would be adolescent hot-takes about their assigned reading, but that’s not the case. Most of the characters are diligent and intelligent students, so their discussion of The Crucible can contain nuance.
Lucas Calhoun, photo courtesy of Nashville Repertory Theatre
John Proctor is the Villain is interesting in that it accepts the conclusion of The Crucible: that suppressed malevolence and envy can find outlet through hysterical witchhunts (literal or metaphorical), no matter the moral claims of the perpetrators. In her play Kimberly Bellflower advances another true statement: that while communities and cultures can be swept away by hysterical witchhunts, they can also hide their heads in the sand, preferring to believe that someone is hysterical or lying than believe that a person they know is actually a villain. Especially if accusations are made by young women against respectable men. Willful complacency can suppress justice as much as mob hysteria. The bias of gender adds layers to this, and the play reveals another troubling aspect to predation and accusation: how should a child respond to an accusation against their father? Emphasizing this, the play is set in 2018, when the #MeToo movement was at its height. In her Director’s Note, Lauren Shouse writes, “John Proctor is the Villain reminds us that the question is not simply whether we will tell the truth. It is whether we will have the courage to listen and act when someone else does.”
One limitation of the play’s interaction with The Crucible is that, while they do try to summarize and contextualize much of their discussion, you really do want to be familiar with that play to fully understand this one. I saw The Crucible a few years ago and recently watched the 90’s film adaptation, so all the commentary made sense, but I brought my sister to the show, and she was unfamiliar with the play. She told me that this lack of context took away some of the impact she felt John Proctor is the Villain expected to have upon her.
Like The Crucible, John Proctor is the Villain is set in a very specific moment in time. However, unlike Arthur Miller’s creation, this is sometimes a weakness: there are too many 2018 specific pop culture references, some of which only certain groups would understand in the first place. There are many references to Taylor Swift lyrics that I’m sure Swifties enjoy, but are lost on the general populace. Now that it’s 2026, teenagers who would be expected to relate to the age-appropriate humor won’t get a lot of the references, since a current Junior in high school was around eight years old in 2018. The play faces the difficulty that the references can’t be updated, since it’s specifically set during the #MeToo moment, so I’m curious about how the film will deal with this aspect of the play.
While Bellflower’s teenager’s references are too specific, I admire her knack for capturing the rhythm and content of teenage conversation. The dialogue bounces organically from subject to subject, constantly interspersed with humor, goofiness, and sincere convictions. The conversations feel so natural, and teenagers’ occasional tendencies to sloganeering allow her to make her points obvious without making the dialogue feel stilted.
L to R: Advika Ravishankar, Aubrey Bagley, Norah Younger, Brityn Ramsey, photo courtesy of Nashville Repertory Theatre
The cast does a great job of embodying teenagers and educators alike: Aubrey Bagley plays Beth Powell, a bubbly character who frequently giggles, and she’s hilarious and wonderful. She made Beth into my favorite character, and I expect to see her on more Nashville stages. Lucas Calhoun plays Carter Smith, who sums up that cool teacher vibe and has great chemistry with the cast. Norah Younger as Shelby Holcomb embodies the inconsistent confidence and suppression of troubled adolescence. She moves her eyes nervously, forcing, then avoiding eye contact.
The set is great, a simple classroom with two doors leading out. There are desks and chairs, beanbag chairs at the front of the stage, with the walls covered with educational posters. At the very front of the stage is a small strip of gravel scattered with leaves. I didn’t notice this portion until the moment it came into use for the one scene set outside the school, and it was perfect, instantly evoking that familiar memory of hanging out outdoors somewhere sipping on cheap sodas. This is a small thing, but it bothered me: the interstitial music as people exited and entered the set was very loud and intense, and cut off abruptly and slightly out of sync with the lights as action began.
This play contains excellent critiques and commentary on many of the issues at work in actual Salem during the witch trials, and with the #MeToo Movement, but there is inconsistency in the pacing and quality of writing, a predictable reveal as well as some stiltedness in the wording of a dramatic monologue. It has several laugh-out-loud moments, and it is certainly more fun than Arthur Miller’s profound but miserably tragic work.
The Nashville Rep will be performing John Proctor is the Villain through October 18th. See their website for tickets and more information.
