Museum Rat #54: Mazu Temple, Meizhou Island
Where do we keep our ornament now?
KARIMAHOct 02, 20261ShareMeizhou Island | Putian, Fujian
Open year round
The gist: a girl born on a Fujian island in 960 became the goddess of the sea, and the shrine her neighbours built for her turned into about five thousand temples worldwide. I'm on residency nearby, so I went.
Hey my beautiful museum rats. Or should I say, this week, temple rats.
I’ll keep it short and sweet, because I know this one is less likely to be somewhere you can visit. I’m in Putian, in China, where I’ve been doing my residency with Rose Blue Studio, and most of you won’t be able to make it over. But I want to tell you about the goddess Mazu and about Meizhou Island.
Mazu was a real person before she was a goddess. She was born Lin Moniang on Meizhou in 960, a fisherman’s daughter, and they called her the Silent Girl because as a baby she never cried. The story goes that she was sitting at her loom one day when she fell into a trance, and in the trance she went out across the water to pull her father and brothers out of a typhoon. Someone woke her before she had finished. She died at twenty-seven, and the islanders built her a shrine.
A thousand years later there are around five thousand Mazu temples in the world, nearly a thousand in Taiwan alone, and every one of them traces itself back to that shrine. She started as the goddess of fishermen and sailors and has since picked up childbirth, drought, flood and epidemic along the way. UNESCO put the belief on its intangible cultural heritage list in 2009.
The island itself is small, about fourteen square kilometres, thirty-eight thousand people, most of them still fishing, and twenty kilometres of beach. Taiwanese pilgrims come over every year to stand where she lived. The shrine is now a very large temple complex built to hold all of them.
I ummed and ahhed this week about putting a temple in Museum Rat, because I usually cover galleries and pop-up exhibitions. But this is very much a cultural pilgrimage, and if a magazine stall in a shopping centre can make the cut, as it did back in June, then so can this.
It got me thinking about the other temples I’ve been to. A temple in Kyoto. The statue of Mary standing above Beirut. The masjid in Istanbul. Places of worship have very often been where ornament gets gathered up and shown to the world, where a community puts its best carving and its best colour and its best gold.
Which made me wonder where that happens in a more secular Britain. Where are our places of ornamentation and adornment now? The new undergrounds, the Elizabeth line with the monumental ceilings or is it our football stadiums? I’m not sure I’ve landed on an answer, but its a fun question to hold.
Museum Rat verdict?
If you ever find yourself in Putian, go. And if you don't, take this as a reminder to wander into a church or a temple or a mosque next time one takes your curious fancy. You don't have to be of that religion. Just go curious and see what you find.

Planning Your Visit
Where: Meizhou Island, Xiuyu District, Putian, Fujian province, China. Reached by ferry from the mainland at Putian.
What: the Meizhou Ancestral Temple, the founding temple of Mazu belief, begun as a shrine in the tenth century and much enlarged since.
When: the big temple fairs happen twice a year, with smaller offerings, incense and lantern processions throughout.
Worth knowing: Mazu belief and customs was inscribed on UNESCO’s intangible cultural heritage list in 2009.
